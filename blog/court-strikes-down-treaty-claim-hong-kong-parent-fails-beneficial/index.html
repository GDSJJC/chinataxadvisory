Court Strikes Down Treaty Claim: Hong Kong Parent Fails Beneficial Ownership Test

Chinese court upholds tax bureau challenge to dividend withholding relief

A newly reported case from September 29, 2022, adds another important datapoint to China’s evolving practice on treaty shopping and beneficial ownership reviews. At the center of the case was a Hong Kong–based holding company that attempted to claim reduced Chinese withholding tax on dividends paid by its mainland subsidiary, only to face pushback from the tax authorities on the grounds that it failed the “beneficial owner” test under the China–Hong Kong tax treaty.

Case Background: Group Structure and Dividend Flows

In this case, a Chinese subsidiary (“China Sub”) was wholly owned by a Hong Kong-incorporated parent company (“HK parent” or the “taxpayer”), which was in turn 100% owned by a Cayman-based legal entity (“Cayman Co”). All three were a part of a foreign-headquartered multinational group operating in the automotive industry.

The holding structure above was set up about eight years ago. On December 30, 2014, the Cayman Co formed the HK parent with a registered capital of RMB 800 million. About two months later, on February 25, 2015, the HK parent established the China Sub with an injected capital of RMB 750 million.

The HK parent had three directors. They were not based in Hong Kong or Mainland China and did not receive compensation from the HK parent. The taxpayer also had three employees in charge of the training operation of the group in Greater China. The active business revenue and income of the HK parent were relatively small.

The China Sub distributed RMB 31 million, RMB 29 million, and RMB 50.5 million of dividends to the HK parent each year between 2016 and 2018, and claimed the preferential 5% dividend withholding rate under the China-HK income tax agreement (“Treaty”) on behalf of the HK parent. Each time the HK parent distributed the China-Sourced dividend directly upstream to Cayman Co during the same year.

In 2019, the tax authorities in charge of the China Sub (“Tax Bureau”) audited the Treaty position described above. The focus of the controversy was whether the HK parent qualified as the beneficial owner of the Chinese dividend. After reviewing the articles of association, the financial statements, the minutes of the board of directors, and other materials and arguments submitted by the HK parent, the Tax Bureau challenged that the answer should be negative. The taxpayer disagreed.

As the requirements and factors in the Chinese beneficial ownership rules have been discussed extensively in prior updates, we will skip the rules and go straight to the position of each side.

Taxpayer’s Position: Legal Form and Limited Operations in Hong Kong

The HK parent argued that it was the beneficial owner of the dividends via the following.

First, it was registered under the laws of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region of the People’s Republic of China (“PRC”). It had the “irrefutable status of a civil and commercial legal subject”. It had the right to invest and carry out other business following the local law and was entitled to make the actual disposal of the investment income received including allocating it to the Cayman Co.

Second, it had a board of directors, hired employees, and run regional training operations out of Hong Kong for the Greater China area.

Third, it obtained the dividend income based on its investment in the China Sub, and fulfilled all the mandated tax filing formalities in the PRC via the China Sub.

Tax Authority’s Position: Lack of Substance and Negative Indicators

The Tax Bureau raised the following points to refute the arguments made by the taxpayer.

  • The registration of the HK parent in the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region of the PRC to meet the organizational form required by law is a “necessary but insufficient” condition for the HK parent to have the status of “beneficial owner”.

  • The HK parent’s legal right to distribute the China-Sourced dividends to the Cayman Co does not conclude its status as a “beneficial owner”.

  • The fact that HK parent had a board of directors and hired employees does not necessarily give rise to “substantive business activities”. Whether the taxpayer carried out substantive business activities should be determined based on a “functional & risk analysis”.

  • The fulfillment of the compliance process by the HK parent via the China Sub is a “necessary but not a sufficient” condition for the Treaty benefit.

  • The board of directors in the HK parent distributed all the Chinese dividends to the Cayman Co in the same year of receipt. While this did not prove that the HK parent had a legal obligation to do so, it triggered a negative factor listed in the Chinese regulations on beneficial ownership.

  • The HK parent only had three employees and no directors based in Hong Kong. Its active income in Hong Kong was insignificant compared with the dividends received from the China Sub, and its operations in Hong Kong were immaterial against the size of the equity investment in the China Sub.

  • The HK parent was established on December 30, 2014 and invested RMB 750 million to establish the China Sub on February 25, 2015. It is inconceivable to complete the complex due diligence and research work behind the significant Chinese investment within 2 months. This, in conjunction with the lack of reinvestment of the Chinese dividend by the HK parent, suggested that the taxpayer did not perform substantive investment management functions in Hong Kong.

At the end, the Tax Bureau concluded that the HK parent did not carry out substantial business operations or investment management activities in Hong Kong and failed to qualify as the beneficial owner of the Chinese dividends. The Treaty benefit was denied.

Commentary: Implications for MNC Dividend Planning

There have been increasing tax audit activities against MNCs receiving dividends from China. The usual challenge from the tax examiners is the qualification of the dividend recipient for the beneficial owner status, which is a prerequisite for enjoying a Chinese treaty withholding tax rate. The Hong Kong tax residency status of the taxpayer in the case did not appear to be an issue, and that is true for many other similar cases in practice.

The Chinese definition of beneficial ownership contains both an economic element and a legal element. Merely arguing that the recipient is a legal owner of the Chinese dividend is unlikely to prevail in the context of the Chinese tax regulations. Instead, the intended beneficial owner has to show that it conducts sufficient activities in the residence jurisdiction, either through commercial operations or investment management functions, in order to demonstrate a sufficiently strong local economic connection. The Chinese tax authorities consider this essential to screen out treaty-shopping arrangements.

This means that during tax planning, MNCs should take a cautious approach and prepare for future challenges by adopting prudent factual patterns. For historical transactions where the facts were final, the right defense approach is critical during negotiations with the tax authorities to seek the most favorable outcome in each situation.
