Changchun Audit Forces $7M Tax Bill Through Valuation Adjustment

Authorities reject income method and impose tax based on land appreciation

A recent transfer pricing dispute in Changchun, Jilin Province sheds light on how Chinese tax authorities evaluate equity transfers involving underperforming assets, and how valuation method selection can dramatically change the tax outcome. In this case, a Hong Kong company (the foreign shareholder) and a local Chinese company (the joint venture partner) established a joint venture in 2007 to develop and operate a shopping mall in Changchun. The Hong Kong shareholder held 65%, while the local partner held 35%.

After years of poor performance, the mall had never generated a profit. In 2017, the foreign shareholder decided to exit the project and transferred its 65% equity interest to the joint venture partner. The parties used a valuation method similar to the income approach, concluding that, given the mall’s bleak outlook, the equity had little value. The Hong Kong shareholder reported no gain and paid no Chinese income tax on the transfer.

Tax Authority Challenge: Asset Value vs. Earnings Potential

The Changchun State Tax Bureau launched an audit of the transaction and took issue with the valuation. While the business itself had little earning potential, the tax officials focused on a single balance sheet item: the land on which the mall was built.

According to the authorities:

  • The land use right was granted at a favorable price by the local government in 2007.

  • Since then, the fair market value of the land had appreciated significantly.

  • This appreciation should have been reflected in the equity’s value, regardless of the JV’s earnings outlook.

The tax bureau rejected the use of the income method in this case and insisted on applying the cost method, which captures the current market replacement value of key assets like land. On this basis, they increased the deemed sale price of the equity interest and imposed approximately USD 7 million in corporate income tax on the foreign seller, with the buyer designated as the withholding agent. The tax was ultimately paid.

Valuation Methods in Chinese Transfer Pricing

Under Chinese transfer pricing regulations, three valuation methods are prescribed for related-party asset transfers:

  • Cost Method – Based on the cost to reproduce or replace the asset under current market conditions.

  • Income Method – Based on the present value of future expected returns.

  • Market Method – Based on comparable market transactions.

In practice, the market method is rarely used due to the difficulty of identifying reliable comparables. The income method is typically favored by Chinese tax authorities, especially where the transferred entity holds off-balance-sheet intangible assets, such as goodwill or brand value. These can be captured through future return projections but are often missed under the cost method.

However, as this Changchun case shows, tax authorities are flexible and outcome-oriented. If a high-value tangible asset is present (such as appreciated real estate), they may revert to the cost method to maximize the tax base, even when the business has no profitability.

Key Messages for Multinational Taxpayers

While the income method has become the general preference for Chinese tax authorities, it is not universally applied. Tax examiners may pivot to the cost method when it serves their audit objectives, particularly in the presence of appreciated assets like land or real estate. Multinational companies are advised to use a blended valuation approach, drawing on both income and cost methods, to build a more robust position.

When valuing equity in a Chinese target, companies should pay special attention to specific high-risk assets such as real estate, intellectual property, or downstream equity holdings. These assets are frequent audit triggers, especially when their book value diverges significantly from fair market value.

The case also raises an important technical issue. The transfer pricing regulations, and by extension, the use of the prescribed valuation methods, apply only to transactions between related parties. In this case, the foreign shareholder and the Chinese JV partner were joint investors in the same company, but not clearly related parties under the transfer pricing rules.

Had the taxpayer challenged this point, it could have undermined the legal basis for using any TP-based valuation method at all. The negotiated price between unrelated parties arguably already incorporated market factors like land appreciation, making the tax adjustment unnecessary.
