Hubei TP Audit Imposes RMB 300M Adjustment on Royalties and Service Fees

Limited-risk manufacturer characterization drives major tax settlement

A new transfer pricing (“TP”) audit case was recently concluded in Hubei Province, China. While the fact pattern is relatively standard, it represents a trend of a Chinese transfer pricing audit in today’s economic environment.

Overview

The Chinese taxpayer (“China Sub”) was established in January 2012 with about RMB 100 million of registered capital and engaged in the production and sales of auto parts. During the first two years of operations, the China Sub incurred operating losses. As the China Sub paid substantial royalties, technical service fees, interest, and other fees to its foreign affiliates during the period, the local tax bureau of Xiaogan City, Hubei Province, suspected that the non-arm’s length nature of these payments led to the operating losses. As the tax office considered the China Sub a limited risk manufacturer from a TP standpoint, aka a single-function entity (“SFE”) in Chinese TP terms, it questioned the authenticity and rationality of the outbound payments. Later, the China Sub perform a self-examination per the request of the tax bureau and maintained that except for a few small items, its TP policy was arm’s length and no self-adjustment was necessary. The tax authorities dropped the case back then.

The case was reopened in August 2019 when the tax bureau observed additional losses at the China Sub. This time investigation was expanded to the 2012-2019 period, again focusing on the intercompany payments of royalty and service fees. The China Sub hired an accounting firm to defend the case. The tax bureau conducted rounds of management interviews and field inspections. It insisted that (i) as an SFE, the China Sub should be ensured a routine profit and not be exposed to market risks and losses; and (ii) the TNMM method should be used to set the targeted profitability level for the China Sub.

In August 2020, the China Sub agreed to the LRM entity characterization and the TNMM methodology, and the issue became the determination of the targeted profit. After eight more meetings between April and August 2021, the two sides agreed to a TNMM comparable set consisting of 27 3rd party companies and a target operating margin of 3.51%. This led to an aggregate upward adjustment in taxable income of more than RMB 300 million. The China Sub settled about USD 15 million of tax and interest after offsetting the adjustment against its historical NOLs.

Takeaways for Multinational Taxpayers

The case offers several important lessons for foreign-headquartered multinationals on how to approach TP audits in China. Chinese TP matters are governed by a ten-year statute of limitations, meaning that an audit can be initiated many years after the underlying transactions occur. As a result, significant TP exposure may remain dormant until near the end of that period. Companies cannot rely on the absence of current tax authority inquiries as evidence of safety; TP risk must be continuously assessed and managed throughout the full limitation window.

The authorities’ primary focus continues to fall on intercompany outbound payments, particularly royalties and service fees. These payments often attract scrutiny not only for their pricing but also for their underlying substance. Auditors typically look beyond arm’s length benchmarking to question the commercial reality of the arrangements: whether the foreign licensor genuinely owns and controls the intellectual property for which it charges royalties, or whether the foreign service provider possesses the personnel, technical capacity, and business infrastructure to deliver the services for which it is compensated. Maintaining detailed, contemporaneous documentation on these points is therefore essential for defending the legitimacy of the payments.

In the case at hand, while the outbound royalties and service fees were the initial trigger for the audit, the tax authority ultimately adopted an entity-level approach rather than focusing on individual transactions. By switching to the TNMM, auditors effectively sidestepped the question of deductibility for each payment and instead imposed a target profit margin for the company as a whole. This methodology can lead to a far larger upward adjustment than the simple disallowance of specific deductions. It underscores the importance of ensuring that the entity’s transfer pricing characterization aligns with its actual commercial operations. If the authorities detect inconsistencies, particularly through interviews with management, they may disregard the taxpayer’s TP documentation and reconstruct the entity’s profile entirely based on their own interpretation of the facts.
