China Introduces Withholding Tax Credit for Foreign Dividend Reinvestment

New policy converts prior deferral into permanent relief—aimed at incentivizing long-term reinvestment amid global capital realignment

On June 27, 2025, China’s Ministry of Finance, State Taxation Administration (“STA”), and Ministry of Commerce jointly issued Announcement [2025] No. 2, introducing a policy aimed at encouraging capital reinvestment by foreign investors in China. Effective from January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2028, the policy allows an eligible foreign investor to claim a withholding tax credit when it reinvests dividends received from a Chinese resident enterprise into qualifying investments within China. The tax credit, calculated as the applicable withholding tax rate multiplied by the reinvested dividend amount, may be used to offset the Chinese withholding tax of the foreign investor on future passive income (e.g., dividends, interest, royalties) derived from the distributing Chinese enterprise. The tax credit is not time-limited and may be carried forward indefinitely, provided certain conditions continue to be met.

On July 31, 2025, the STA issued Bulletin 18 to outline the procedures for securing the incentive. More importantly, the accompanying interpretation provides illustrative scenarios and example-based clarifications to address practical questions on how the incentive should be applied in practice.

Withholding Tax Credit Based on Dividend Reinvestment

The new policy provides that a foreign investor who directly reinvests dividends from a Chinese resident enterprise into a qualifying direct equity investment may claim a tax credit equal to the applicable withholding tax rate multiplied by the amount reinvested. This credit can then be applied against Chinese withholding tax imposed on future passive income from the same profit-distributing enterprise.

The default tax credit rate is 10%, reflecting the statutory withholding rate under China’s domestic tax law. However, if the foreign investor qualifies for a reduced withholding rate under an applicable tax treaty, the treaty rate may be used in computing the credit.

Eligible Reinvestment Scenarios

For a foreign company such as Company A to qualify for the withholding tax credit, the reinvestment must take one of three recognized forms, subject to detailed procedural requirements such as fund flow and disclosure. One option is to reinvest the dividends into Company B, the original profit distributor in China, by injecting the dividends back into Company B through an increase in its registered capital or a contribution to its capital reserve. Another approach is for Company A to use the dividends received from Company B to establish a new Chinese resident enterprise, Company C. Alternatively, Company A may use the distributed profits from Company B to acquire an equity interest in an unrelated, non-listed Chinese resident enterprise, Company D, through a third-party transaction.

Mechanics of Credit Utilization

A key condition for preserving the tax credit is that the foreign investor must hold the reinvestment for a continuous period of at least five years (60 months). If the foreign investor retains the reinvestment for this period and later exits, the previously deferred withholding tax on the original dividend becomes payable at the time of exit. The withholding tax credit can then be used to offset this tax liability.

For example, if Company A, a foreign investor, receives a dividend of RMB 100 million from Company B in China and reinvests the full amount into a qualifying structure, and the applicable withholding tax rate is 10%, a RMB 10 million tax credit becomes available to Company A. If the reinvestment is held for more than five years, the RMB 10 million in deferred withholding tax that becomes payable upon exit may be fully offset by the credit, provided that Company A has not already used the tax credit to offset any passive income received from Company B during the holding period.

Conversely, if the investment is withdrawn before the five-year threshold is met, the deferred tax becomes immediately payable, and the associated credit is disqualified. Any credit previously used would be subject to clawback.

Takeaways

Competing Global Investment Pressures

China introduced this new incentive against the backdrop of intensifying geopolitical tensions and evolving global trade dynamics—particularly the trend in U.S. policy toward encouraging the reshoring of foreign investment. In this environment, China has responded by strengthening its tax policy tools to retain and attract foreign capital, positioning this incentive as a countermeasure to reverse or slow the outward migration of investment fund flows.

This policy builds upon the earlier withholding tax deferral regime first introduced under Caishui [2018] No. 102 and further clarified in STA Bulletin 2018 No. 53. The new policy represents a material enhancement: instead of mere deferral, it provides a tax credit mechanism. If reinvested profits are retained in China for at least five years, and subsequently repatriated, the amount may qualify for full exemption from Chinese withholding tax—transforming what was previously a temporary deferral into a permanent tax saving. The incentive is designed to influence capital allocation decisions within established foreign-invested enterprises, promoting long-term reinvestment over repatriation.

Monitoring the Tax Credit Account

From an implementation standpoint, once the required procedural steps are completed, the foreign investor is deemed to hold a tax credit account. However, this credit remains provisional until the five-year reinvestment requirement is satisfied.

Prior to the five-year mark, the tax credit gives rise to deferred tax assets and liabilities under standard accounting frameworks. Accordingly, any investment decision involving repatriation, restructuring, or disposal of China-based operations should take into account the recognition and potential reversal of these deferred tax positions. Such considerations are essential to accurately reflect the timing and magnitude of tax impacts on the investor’s financial statements.

During the reinvestment period, the account must be closely monitored. Ongoing tracking is therefore essential to ensure compliance and to inform the timing of any repatriation or internal restructuring. Once the five-year threshold is met, the credit crystallizes into a permanent exemption from Chinese withholding tax—providing a definitive tax benefit.

Cross-Border Coordination and Pillar Two Impacts

In parallel, investors should evaluate how this Chinese tax credit interacts with their global tax profile. While the credit may reduce future Chinese tax liabilities, its after-tax value will depend on a number of external factors—such as the investor’s home jurisdiction rules governing foreign tax credit recognition, anti-deferral regimes, and interaction with the OECD’s Pillar Two global minimum tax framework.

A jurisdiction-specific analysis is therefore crucial to determine whether the incentive delivers meaningful group-level tax savings. In some structures, the nominal Chinese tax benefit may be partially or entirely offset by increased tax exposure elsewhere—underscoring the importance of holistic planning and careful cross-border coordination.
