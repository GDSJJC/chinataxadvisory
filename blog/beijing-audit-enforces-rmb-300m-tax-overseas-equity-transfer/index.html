Beijing Audit Enforces RMB 300M Tax on Overseas Equity Transfer

Share swap arrangement denied tax deferral under strict PRC scrutiny

Recently, the Shunyi District Taxation Bureau (“SDTB”) in Beijing acted upon a shareholder status update for a Chinese company under its charge to collect nearly RMB 300 million (about USD 45 million) of enterprise income tax on a transfer of Chinese equity interest. The case wasn’t controversial in tax treatment but reflects the trend that (a) the local tax authorities in China are increasingly scrutinizing overseas equity transfers for foreign-invested enterprises, and (b) have established a mature administration system for detection and collection.

Audit Discovery and Transaction Review

In January 2023, the SDTB used the government IT system to screen and analyze the foreign-invested enterprises in its district and found that the shareholder composition of a local company (“target company”) had changed. The SDTB previously understood that the target company was held by four corporate shareholders. Two of them were non-residents and were related, with each holding 14% of the target company. During the check, the SDTB noticed that one foreign shareholder (“seller”) no longer possessed any shareholding, while the other foreign shareholder (“buyer”) increased its ownership to 28%.

The local tax officials had not received tax filings indicating a shareholder change, so they requested interviews with the target company’s personnel and verified that an equity transfer transaction indeed happened not long ago. Through the target company, the SDTB contacted the seller and the buyer and obtained the equity transfer agreement (“ETA”). According to the ETA, in January 2022, the seller sold its 14% equity interest in the target company to the related buyer at a price exceeding 5 billion RMB (about USD 750 million), and the parties designated the buyer to handle all Chinese tax matters of the transaction as the withholding agent.

The SDTB focused on whether the price was fair, and whether the tax basis was verifiable so that the right amount of income tax could be collected. Given the relationship between the seller and the buyer, the tax authorities initially required an asset appraisal report for the target company to determine whether the transfer price was at arm’s length. The buyer explained that no 3rd party asset appraisal report was prepared, but it could provide an internal analysis to substantiate the valuation. The SDTB used its own data to assess the asset composition of the target company in the past two years, and leveraged third-party information sources to check the transaction prices at which other shareholders of the target company transferred their equity interests in recent years. After this exercise, the SDTB got comfortable that the transfer price derived from the target company’s internal valuation analysis was reasonable.

The tax officials also requested the previous contract and payment voucher in connection with the seller’s historical acquisition of the equity interest in the target company to confirm the tax basis. After reviewing the submitted documents, the SDTB accepted the cost basis figure and determined the amount of enterprise income tax to be withheld.

Chinese tax regulations stipulate that when income tax on an equity transfer is settled by a withholding agent, the tax should be declared and paid within 7 days from the occurrence of the obligation, or a late payment interest will arise. In this case, the buyer experienced cash flow difficulties, and the SDTB agreed to receive the withholding tax payments by installment. After performing a temporary tax registration in China for the transaction, the buyer submitted a withholding tax filing and settled the RMB 300 million of income tax in batches. In addition, the buyer also made a separate discharge for a late payment interest five days after all income tax was settled.

Practical Implications for Multinationals

This case provides a valuable reference for the prevailing tax administration practices regarding equity transfers of foreign-invested enterprises in China.

To obtain information on the overseas transfer of Chinese equity interests, the local tax authorities in China have established a ledger management system for foreign-invested enterprises in their respective districts to track the status updates of foreign shareholders and monitor major changes in operations and management teams. A material shift in a foreign-invested enterprise’s business model or leadership team may imply a change of ownership outside the Chinese border. When a red flag is identified, the tax authorities usually contact the foreign-invested enterprises first and seek interviews with the local management personnel. After verifying the existence of an unreported equity transfer transaction, the tax authorities will take corresponding collection measures.

Given the establishment of the above system, foreign shareholders should not assume that an unreported equity transfer will remain undetected. Taxpayers are advised to communicate with the competent tax authorities before or simultaneously with the closing of the transaction and take the initiative to negotiate the core parameters with the tax officials such as the transaction price, the tax basis, the tax filing and payment process, the temporary tax registration, the tax payment timeframe, etc. An alignment in advance, even if orally reached, will materially reduce tax dispute and compliance risks later.

In this case, the ETA designated the responsibility for handling income tax matters to the buyer as the withholding agent, and the tax regulations specified a 7-day withholding tax settlement deadline for such a scenario. The timeline is tight, and this underscores the importance of communicating with the tax authorities sooner rather than later.

Where the buyer and seller are affiliates, the tax authorities scrutinize whether the transaction price reflects the market value of the target enterprise. To increase the chance of acceptance, a common practice is to engage a qualified third-party valuation firm with a license to practice in the Chinese market to issue a valuation report on the target company. In this case, no third-party appraisal report was prepared, but the tax authorities agreed to the value adopted by the taxpayer after reviewing the internal valuation analysis performed by the taxpayer and doing its own homework. This illustrates that the use of third-party valuation reports, while still recommended as a best practice for many situations, is not mandatory from a strictly legal standpoint. An internal valuation can still work if it is robust.

Tax authorities are looking at not only the price of the transaction but also the historical cost basis used in the income tax computation. In this case, the tax authorities required proof of payment for the seller’s previous acquisition of the target company, and this demonstrates the importance of keeping payment records in related party transfers. Some taxpayers tend to be laxer when dealing with cash settlements and journal entries when it comes to intercompany transactions, and this may create tax implications for determining historical tax costs when the same assets are transferred again in the future. The best practice from the Chinese income tax standpoint is to always keep the historical payment proof and the journal entries on record.

Since the buyer and the seller were related, the seller had no concerns about disclosing historical costs to the buyer here. However, if the buyer and the seller are non-affiliated, in practice, the seller is often reluctant to disclose the historical cost to the buyer. In such a case, it is more appropriate to provide for the seller to make the tax declaration in the ETA when feasible rather than for the buyer to do so as the withholding agent. In addition, when the ETA designates the seller to handle the income tax declaration and settlement for the equity transfer, the regulations do not stipulate a fixed tax payment deadline, and the tax settlement timeframe can be negotiated with the local tax authorities. Therefore, the risk of late payment fees will be reduced.

In summary, the transfers of Chinese equity interests by foreign shareholders are heavily tracked and the process of doing so is getting more standardized. The case above exemplifies some key issues that taxpayers should consider for mitigating tax risks.
