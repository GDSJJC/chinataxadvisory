Latest Indirect Share Transfer Case in Beijing

Offshore holding structure recast as direct transfer under PRC rules

Recently, a tax audit case involving an indirect equity transfer took place in Beijing’s Shunyi District. In this case, a non-resident enterprise (an offshore holding company or OHC) purchased 12% of the shares of a Chinese resident enterprise (“onshore target”) in 2024. The OHC itself was also the subject of another equity transaction that occurred offshore. The consideration for both equity transactions was highly correlated, with the onshore equity purchase agreement directly referencing the consideration in the offshore equity transfer agreement, and this drew the attention of the tax authorities. The tax officials audited the offshore equity transaction and ultimately levied approximately USD 3 million in Corporate Income Tax (“CIT”) in accordance with China’s indirect transfer rules.

Audit Findings

The OHC was established in 2020 outside China, and its primary activity during the following four years was the acquisition of shares in the onshore target, with minimal other assets besides the equity investment. Before completing the recent onshore equity transaction, the OHC had already acquired 88% of the onshore target’s shares, and after purchasing the remaining 12% in 2024 (“onshore acquisition”), the onshore target became a wholly-owned subsidiary of the OHC.

Simultaneously, the OHC’s parent company (“Parent”) signed another share transfer agreement, stipulating that immediately after closing the onshore acquisition, the Parent would sell 100% of the OHC’s shares to an unrelated buyer (“offshore transfer”). The onshore acquisition agreement disclosed the parallel offshore transfer transaction and prescribed that the purchase price for the 12% equity of the onshore target would be 12% of the OHC’s sale price as per the offshore transfer agreement.

In the offshore transfer transaction, both the Parent and the unrelated buyer were non-resident enterprises. The parties agreed to transfer 100% of the OHC’s shares for a consideration of CNY 1.25 billion, with a special clause allowing for an adjustment of the transaction price by up to CNY 300 million if certain specified events occurred within two to three years. This adjustment clause made the transaction price in the offshore equity transfer agreement variable, which in turn led to variability in the price in the onshore equity acquisition agreement.

For the onshore equity acquisition, the OHC engaged a Chinese financial agent to complete the necessary PRC tax filings. Since the target company in this equity transaction was a Chinese resident enterprise, under Chinese law, both parties to the transaction were required to pay a stamp duty at the rate of 0.05% based on the transaction price. Due to the variability in the purchase consideration described above, the financial agent contacted the International Taxation Division of the Shunyi District Tax Bureau by phone in May 2024, and sought consultation on how the stamp duty should be computed and filed for the onshore equity transaction.

During the phone consultation, the officials from the International Taxation Division requested a copy of the onshore equity transfer agreement. Upon receiving and reviewing the agreement, the officials quickly identified the clauses related to the parallel offshore equity transfer. The tax authorities then requested additional information on the second transaction and initiated a tax audit against the Parent.

At the end of the tax investigation, the tax officials concluded that the primary business activity of the OHC was investment holding. The company had no employees and had not conducted any substantial business operations. Apart from holding a small amount of liquid assets, the OHC possessed no other tangible assets, and its value primarily derived from the onshore target that it controlled. According to Article 1 of the STA Announcement 2015-7, the Parent’s transfer of the OHC’s equity was deemed to lack a reasonable business purpose and constitute an indirect transfer of the onshore target’s equity to avoid Chinese CIT. Therefore, following Article 47 of the PRC Corporate Income Tax Law, the tax officials recast this indirect transfer as a direct transfer of the onshore target, and imposed a Chinese CIT of approximately USD 3 million on the Parent.

Key Takeaways

In the context of Chinese tax laws and regulations, an indirect transfer refers to the situation where a foreign enterprise indirectly disposes of assets within China by transferring the equity of a company held offshore. In such transactions, the transferor, transferee, and the company being transferred are all foreign enterprises, and the entire arrangement occurs outside China. This makes it challenging for Chinese tax authorities to know about such transactions, putting announcements by foreign-listed companies, financial reports of multinational corporations, or media coverage as the main sources of information to detect relevant clues.

An unusual aspect of this case lies in the fact that the tax authority obtained transaction clues through the OHC’s financial agent, who consulted the tax office on stamp duty matters. Firstly, within the tax authority, the department responsible for stamp duty is typically the Property and Behavior Tax Division, not the International Tax Division (which was in charge of indirect transfers), indicating that the financial agent contacted the incorrect department. Secondly, Chinese stamp duty law explains how to pay stamp duty in cases where the transaction price is uncertain: the dutiable contract should be registered first, with the initial stamp duty paid based on a tentative estimate of consideration for the contract, and the final stamp duty calculated based on the actual contract amount upon settlement. Therefore, the consultation wasn’t that necessary; instead, proper tax treatment should have been determined based on a full legal analysis. Finally, conducting a tax risk assessment on the legal contracts and transaction structure in equity transactions is crucial. This case highlights the insufficient understanding of Chinese indirect transfer rules by the parties involved, as well as the lack of thorough review of the contractual terms from a Chinese tax perspective, leading to inadequate anticipation and control over subsequent tax adjustment risks.
