Hubei Audit Revives 30-Year-Old Consumption Tax Liability

Extraordinary recovery period and NAO intervention reshape enforcement

In June 2024, a publicly listed company in mainland China (“Parent”) disclosed in a filing to the securities exchange that its former subsidiary (“Subsidiary”) received a tax notice from the in-charge tax authorities from the Zhijiang City of Hubei Province, requiring the payment of approximately USD 12 million in unpaid consumption tax. This notice pertains to the period from January 1, 1994, to October 31, 2009, during which Subsidiary failed to report and settle the consumption tax. Although Parent had sold all its shares in Subsidiary to a third party in 2020, the share transfer agreement stipulated that Parent would be responsible for any back taxes assessed for periods before the transfer if the tax authorities pursued Subsidiary for unpaid or underpaid taxes.

Core Tax Issue

Subsidiary’s principal business involves the production of Chinese liquor (Baijiu). According to Chinese consumption tax regulations, liquor manufacturers are required to pay consumption tax. On a high level, it is calculated using a combination of ad valorem (based on sales value) and quantity-based methods, with the formula: Tax Payable = Sales Amount × Ad Valorem Rate + Sales Quantity × Specific Rate. The assessment in this case mainly relates to Subsidiary’s unpaid consumption tax, calculated based on this formula.

Unprecedent Statute of Limitations (“SOL”)

A notable aspect of this case is the 30-year recovery period for the consumption tax, far exceeding the usual 3 to 5 years. According to Article 52 of the “Law of the People’s Republic of China on the Administration of Tax Collection,” tax authorities can pursue unpaid or underpaid taxes within three years due to errors by the taxpayer or withholding agent, with a possible extension to five years, for matters not related to transfer pricing. For tax evasion or tax fraud, there is no limitation period for tax recovery. Although the tax notice does not explicitly state this, it implies that Subsidiary’s conduct may have been classified as tax evasion, allowing for an indefinite recovery period.

Late Payment Interest

Another issue accompanying the 30-year recovery period is the calculation of late payment interest, which in China typically amounts to an annual interest rate of about 18.3% for matters not related to transfer pricing. If the tax at issue dates back to the mid-1990s, Subsidiary could face an interest payment far exceeding the USD 12 million of consumption tax owed. Consequently, how the tax authorities handle and calculate these surcharges in this case will be closely watched. The case remains unresolved as of now, with ongoing discussions between the two sides.

Step-up in Tax Enforcement

This case illustrates that China’s tax enforcement has evolved from the previous approach of tax auditors working in isolation to a more coordinated effort involving other government authorities. According to the tax authorities, the issuance of the tax notice to Subsidiary was prompted by the National Audit Office (“NAO”) during a separate review of the tax case, which mandated the tax authorities to pursue the unpaid tax according to standard procedures. In practice, local tax authorities might exhibit some flexibility concerning tax matters not clearly defined by regulations in the absence of NAO’s participation. However, the approach of the NAO differs from that of the tax authorities, with the NAO likely to bring to light any non-compliance issues based on legal interpretations and refer them to the tax authorities for enforcement. The tax authorities need to report back to the NAO on how these matters are resolved.

Oversight Role of the NAO

The NAO in China reports to the State Council and operates independently of the tax authorities. It is responsible for supervising and auditing national fiscal revenues, public funding, and economic activities under government administration, including tax audits. The NAO does not typically audit individual taxpayers directly but instead audits the operations of tax authorities to ensure that tax collection is conducted by the law and regulations. It also re-examines previously audited tax cases. In its audit report on fiscal revenues dated June 25, 2024, the NAO criticized Chinese tax and customs authorities for failing to properly collect fiscal revenues, citing lax enforcement, systemic loopholes leading to tax losses, and the artificial adjustment of collection schedules, thereby impacting the accuracy of fiscal revenues. With the NAO’s involvement and oversight, local tax authorities are expected to intensify their tax inspections and enforcement efforts in the coming months.

Concluding Note

Considering the current tax enforcement environment, Chinese taxpayers should prioritize legal compliance in their tax affairs. Promises made by local tax officials or private relationships cannot guarantee that a company will not face retrospective tax audits and adjustments in the future. For actions deemed as tax evasion, tax authorities can pursue tax recovery indefinitely. As a result, only tax positions backed by a solid legal foundation can effectively reduce the risks associated with subsequent tax adjustments, and only defenses rooted in law can successfully navigate the increasingly rigorous tax inspections that may arise in the future.
