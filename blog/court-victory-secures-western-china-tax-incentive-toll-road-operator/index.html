Court Victory Secures Western China Tax Incentive for Toll Road Operator

Yulin compensation reclassified as operating revenue to preserve 15% EIT rate

A recent Chinese tax litigation case over the Western Region Tax Incentive (“Western Incentive”) sheds light on a question that extends far beyond one regional policy: how taxpayers qualify for preferential treatments tied to industry catalogues. Though the dispute centered on this specific western incentive, the legal reasoning carries wider implications for other regimes that hinge on the Encouraged Industry Catalogue or similar instruments. For multinational groups navigating China’s complex incentive framework, the case offers timely insights into managing compliance risks and defending incentive claims under increasing scrutiny.

Case Background: Western Incentive Framework

In April 2020, the Chinese government issued Bulletin 23, extending the Western Incentive by another ten years, i.e., to the end of 2030. The core part of the Western Incentive is a preferential 15% enterprise income tax (“EIT”) rate to a qualified business that derives a “threshold” percentage of revenue from “encouraged industry sectors” in the Western region. The “encouraged industry sectors” are listed in an industry catalogue published by the government and updated periodically. The “threshold” was previously defined to be 70% but is now lowered to 60% in Bulletin 23.

The renewal of the Western Incentive reflects the desire of the central government to develop the Western Region, which consists of 13 provincial areas lagging behind the rest of China in economic progress. While the rules seem straightforward, complications arise often in reality. There was a fairly recent court case concerned with the application of the Western Incentive rules, and the taxpayer won the case after the litigation. The winning strategy serves as a useful reference in similar cases.

The taxpayer was a foreign-invested enterprise in Yulin, Guangxi province, and operated a toll road as well as the ancillary service facilities under a contract with the local government. The operating period under the contract was between 1997 and 2026 at the time of signing. In 2013, the municipal government in Yulin requested to annul the contract due to a change of city development plan. After multiple rounds negotiations, the contract was cancelled and the Yulin government paid approximately RMB 300 million to the taxpayer as compensation for the early termination of the contract (“municipal compensation”).

Disputed Issue: Compensation vs. Qualified Revenue

The issue under debate was the applicable EIT rate for the year when the taxpayer received the municipal compensation. At the time of contract termination, the taxpayer was enjoying the 15% EIT rate under the Western Incentive and wanted to apply the same rate to the municipal compensation income. The tax authorities disagreed. They argued that to enjoy the Western Incentive, the taxpayer must derive at least 70% (the percentage applicable at the time) of its annual revenue from business operations in an encouraged sector. While operating a toll road was an “encouraged business” under the relevant industry catalogue, the key question was whether the RMB 300 million municipal compensation could be viewed as business revenue from toll road operation. The tax authorities classified it as a “one-time penalty” derived from the unilateral annulment of the contract by the Yulin municipal income, which was unrelated to the toll road business. Such a classification and the resulting dilution caused the taxpayer to miss the minimum threshold of qualified revenue for its whole business. Therefore, EIT at the regular 25% rate instead of the 15% under the Western Incentive was imposed for that year.

The two sides could NOT reach an agreement after an administrative appeal so litigation in court ensued. The taxpayer argued that the municipal compensation was in lieu of the future operating revenues from the toll road business. The fact that future revenue was accelerated to the current year should not change its tax characterization. The taxpayer’s alternative argument was on “consistency.” While having paid EIT on the municipal compensation, the taxpayer was also asked to pay business tax (the prevailing type of turnover tax at the time that is now replaced by VAT) on it. The taxpayer argued that if the tax authorities treat the municipal compensation as a penalty and non-operating in nature, no business tax should have been levied.

The court was sympathetic to the taxpayer’s arguments under the two-pronged approach and ruled that the tax authorities did not have convincing legal evidence to deny the Western Incentive to the taxpayer. It decided that the taxpayer was entitled to use a 15% EIT rate for the toll road compensation during the year and the tax authorities should provide a full refund of the tax and late payment interest to the taxpayer.

Court’s Reasoning and Broader Lessons

The case highlights the importance of strategy in tax controversy. In the case, EIT and business tax were collected by different local tax authorities in the city of Yulin where the taxpayer was located. The different tax departments in charge of EIT and business tax collection did not coordinate with each other and acted out of the self-interests of maximizing tax collection. The taxpayer capitalized on this inconsistency when arguing for its case, and depicted a situation where the local tax authorities should either give up their EIT position to retain the business tax collection or collect EIT at 25% but give up the business tax revenue. Once the court recognized the self-contradiction in the positions of the local tax authorities, it felt obliged to agree with the taxpayer.

Outside the Western region, similar tax incentives aiming to stimulate high-priority industries are being rolled out this year. The most notable one is a 15% EIT rate for companies that carry out substantive operations from an encouraged business in the Hainan Free Trade Area. To qualify a Hainan-based business needs to derive at least 60% of its revenue out of encouraged industries as defined by designated catalogues. The controversy above in the Western Incentive could be encountered by businesses relying on the Hainan incentive, which also sets a minimum percentage threshold for qualified revenues each year. If a dispute happens with the local tax authority, the case in Yulin may serve as a useful reference.
