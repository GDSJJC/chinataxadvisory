Sichuan Court Overturns $4M Tax Penalty on Procedural Grounds

Local bureau loses twice as conflict of interest taints audit hearing

A recent tax audit case in central China culminated in tax litigations at the district court and the appellate court. The tax authorities lost to the taxpayer in both judicial proceedings due to a procedural deviation from the relevant Chinese laws and regulations, and the taxpayer was waived of over US$ 4 million in administrative fines. The case shows that technical arguments in conjunction with the right defense strategy can help a taxpayer win a tough audit, even in a smaller 3rd tier city in China.

Background of the Case

A Chinese real estate developer (”the taxpayer”) in Guanghan city of Sichuan province was audited for a 2012 share acquisition, in which the taxpayer purchased the equity interests of a Chinese target company (”the target”) for cash. Some of the target’s shareholders were Chinese individuals and realized capital gains from their dispositions.

During the audit, the local tax investigation bureau’s main challenge was that the taxpayer failed to withhold and remit Chinese individual income tax (”IIT”) on behalf of the individual shareholders of the target. In February 2018, the local tax investigation bureau issued to the taxpayer a written notice imposing an administrative penalty equal to 100% of the supposedly under-withheld IIT.

Upon the taxpayer’s request, a public hearing was held in March 2018, in which the taxpayer presented its evidence and argued why the penalty was not justified. After the hearing, the reviewing committee of the local tax administrations upheld the decision of the tax investigation team and imposed approximately US$ 4 million of fines on the taxpayer. The taxpayer objected and sued the tax authorities in the local District Court (”the Court”).

Court Proceedings

Under the PRC Administrative Litigation Law, before the government authorities make administrative decisions that can materially impact a relevant party, such as ordering the closure of business operations, revoking a business license, or imposing a significant amount of administrative penalty, the relevant party should have the right to a public hearing. The hearing should be led by a government official who is not a part of the investigation in question to reduce potential prejudice.

The Court recognized that the local tax authorities organized the public hearing and, in this aspect, followed the legal requirement. However, during the court proceedings, it was challenged by the taxpayer that the local tax official presiding over the public hearing also participated in the tax audit and interviewed the management team of the target previously. After reviewing the facts and hearing arguments on both sides, the Court agreed that this personnel choice by the local tax authorities tainted the public hearing process as it created a potential conflict of interest.

The Court was also dissatisfied with the level of evidence that the tax authorities submitted in defense. It indicated that for a case involving material administrative penalties, the local tax authorities should follow the “beyond a reasonable doubt” rather than the “preponderance of the evidence” standard to prove that the taxpayer’s behavior merits the magnitude of the penalty treatment handed down. Because of the procedural violation as well as the insufficient strength of evidence on the side of the government auditors, the Court ruled in favor of the taxpayer.

The local tax authorities appealed this case with the intermediate court at the municipal level and were defeated once again on July 29, 2019.

Key Insights

The Sichuan case highlights the importance of taxpayer’s active defense in the face of belligerent tax audits. In many locations, the in-charge tax officials and auditors lack familiarity with regulations and sometimes bend government procedures to gain an upper hand over the taxpayers. Companies as a result often received requests that lacked a legal basis and yet faced pressure to give in due to the perceived authoritativeness of tax officials’ viewpoints.

In the Sichuan case, instead of writing off the $4 million of penalty as a cost of doing business in China, the taxpayer was willing to fully utilize the various relief measures (e.g., public hearing, court litigations) prescribed by Chinese laws and regulations to protect its rights. Given that the fiscal situations of many local governments may deteriorate in the coming months, the right approach and mentality will become more important than ever in defending against aggressive positions from the local auditors.
