Hefei Indirect Transfer Case Triggers $100M PRC Tax Liability

Local tax bureau disregards offshore structure, designates buyer as withholding agent

A recent indirect transfer case reported in Hefei, Anhui Province, located in central China, offers a fresh perspective on how Chinese tax authorities apply the indirect transfer rules, particularly when a domestic buyer is involved.

Case Background

The transaction involved an equity transfer formalized in a share purchase agreement (“SPA”) signed in August 2018. The seller was a British Virgin Islands (“BVI”) entity ultimately owned by a Taiwanese parent company. The target of the sale was a Hong Kong company (“Target”), while the buyer was a Chinese enterprise (“Buyer”) based in Hefei. Under the terms of the transaction, the BVI Seller transferred a 49% equity interest in the Hong Kong Target to the Chinese Buyer for cash consideration.

While many indirect transfer cases involve only offshore entities, this case was somewhat atypical in that the Buyer was a Chinese resident company. Even so, both the Seller and the Target were non-resident enterprises. As such, under general sourcing rules, the Seller’s capital gains would normally be treated as foreign-sourced income, unless the gains are recharacterized as Chinese-sourced under China’s indirect transfer regime. In this case, the Seller and its tax advisor initially took the position that the transaction should not trigger tax liability in China. However, the fact that the Buyer was in China necessitated the remittance of foreign exchange funds from the PRC to the Seller in BVI as payment of consideration. The large amount of outbound foreign currency movement attracted the attention of the local Chinese tax authorities in Hefei.

Tax Investigation and Ruling

The local tax officials requested disclosure information from the transacting parties and concluded that the Target’s net value was principally derived from a lower tier 100% Chinese Subsidiary (”WFOE”), which happened to be incorporated in the city of Hefei as well. The local tax authorities challenged the Seller that the Target lacked sufficient commercial substance in its jurisdiction of incorporation, i.e., Hong Kong, and its corporate form should be pierced for PRC income tax purposes. The interesting part was that the Target in Hong Kong also owned an operating branch in Taiwan with approximately $50 million of branch net assets. The tax officials apparently did not allow the substance in the Taiwan branch to be attributed to the Target for the purpose of determining whether the Target’s corporate identity should be respected from the Chinese income tax perspective.

After rounds of negotiation, the Hefei tax authorities, under the blessing of the STA, ruled that the sale of Target by the Seller did not meet the business purpose requirement and should be disregarded under China’s indirect transfer regulations. Therefore, the Seller was asked to pay Chinese income tax on gains attributable to its pro rata share in the WFOE. The Chinese Buyer was designated to be the withholding agent. The tax obligation was deemed to arise on Aug 31, 2018, the closing date in the SPA. The Seller accepted the outcome and settled approximately US$ 100 million of Chinese income tax.

Key Reminders for M&A Deals

The case suggests that the Chinese tax authorities continue to enforce the indirect transfer rules vigorously. The Chinese indirect transfer regulations’ tax implications should be analyzed thoroughly in any kind of M&A transactions where a non-Chinese target owns PRC operations or assets directly or through lower tier Chinese subsidiaries.

In this case, if the WFOE were located in a place outside the Anhui province, the level of complexities will rise significantly as the local tax authorities in charge of the WFOE could also claim themselves, rather than the Hefei tax authorities, to be the rightful party entitled to the tax collection. These uncertainties should be adequately taken into account when structuring the M&A deals and negotiating the SPA.
