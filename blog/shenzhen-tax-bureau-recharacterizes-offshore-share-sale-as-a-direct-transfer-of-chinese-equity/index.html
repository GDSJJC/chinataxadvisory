Shenzhen Tax Bureau Recharacterizes Offshore Share Sale as a Direct Transfer of Chinese Equity

A routine business registration change exposes an offshore holding company with no economic substance, leading to tax recovery of more than RMB 24 million on a RMB 240 million transaction under Announcement 2015 No. 7.

What happened

In August 2024, a Shenzhen district bureau flagged an anomaly: a Chinese operating company changed its name, switched scope from steel processing to medical aesthetics and TCM wellness, and replaced its entire management — yet its offshore Hong Kong parent had not changed at all.

Tracing the chain, the bureau found the foreign seller had sold 100% of the Hong Kong holder to another non-resident in early 2024 for RMB 240 million. The holder's only real asset was its stake in the Chinese company — some 99% of assets, with no operating revenue or expenses of its own.

Treated as a pure holding platform, the sale was recharacterized as a direct transfer of Chinese equity, with EIT, surcharges and penalties totaling over RMB 24 million.

The governing framework: Announcement 2015 No. 7

The power comes from art. 47 EIT Law (GAAR). Announcement 7 (effective Feb 3, 2015) lets authorities look through an offshore sale and tax it as a direct sale of Chinese assets where the offshore structure lacks reasonable commercial purpose — typically where value derives mainly from Chinese taxable property and the vehicle has no real substance.

On recharacterization the gain is China-source, 10% non-resident rate. Withholding falls on the buyer as payer (art. 37); if not withheld, recovery lies against the seller (art. 39).

Testing the facts

What drew attention was the onshore overhaul, not the offshore sale — the incongruity the risk model flags. On substance, a vehicle that merely holds shares fails the commercial-purpose test.

The buyer agreed by contract to bear any tax and did not withhold. That binds the parties but does not discharge the seller's statutory duty — so liability fell back on the seller, then circled to the buyer via indemnity as an unbudgeted cost.

Implications

The seller had other Chinese operations distributing dividends — future outbound flows that require tax/FX clearance supplied leverage, likely via cross-jurisdiction coordination. Buyers that only withhold want to close exposure and secure stepped-up basis (withhold, escrow, or report); buyers that agree to bear tax must price and allocate the risk up front.

Recommendations

  • Sellers: test single-purpose holders for substance well before sale; assume a pure holder will be taxed as direct.
  • Buyers: diligence indirect-transfer exposure, reflect in price / funded escrow + indemnity.
  • Both: conspicuous onshore changes (name, scope, management) invite scrutiny even where the registered shareholder is unchanged.
