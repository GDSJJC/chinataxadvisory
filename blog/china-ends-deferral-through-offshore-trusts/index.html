China Ends Deferral Through Offshore Trusts: Annual Tax on Undistributed Income, 90 Days to Clean Up History

Effective 24 July 2026, the funding, yearly growth, and exit of offshore trusts are brought within the existing 20 percent tax categories, closing the deferral that offshore trust structures have long provided. For pre-2026 exposure, the 90-day window running from implementation (22 October 2026) is the deadline that matters: specified historic matters settled by that date escape late-payment surcharge, but the tax itself remains due in full.

Highlights

Three points stand out in this alert. First, the new rules bring the lifecycle of an offshore trust generally within the existing individual income tax system: a resident individual's transfer of property into an offshore trust is a taxable disposal taxed at 20 percent; the income of the trust and its controlled overseas entities is attributed to the resident individual year by year whether or not it is distributed; and distributions, termination, exit, and death have become chargeable events. Second, the 90-day no-surcharge window (22 October 2026, counted by excluding the implementation day) is the transitional filing deadline the reform creates: specified historic matters settled by that date escape late-payment surcharge, but not the tax itself, and once the window closes the ordinary surcharge and evasion-penalty rules of the Tax Collection and Administration Law apply again. Third, from 1 January 2026 resident funding and trust-year income are taxed under the announcements, with the 2026 annual return due between 1 March and 30 June 2027, and compliance work should accordingly shift from a one-off historic clean-up to a standing discipline of annual filing and record-keeping.

Background: A Grey Zone That Survived on Vague Rules

For years, offshore trusts sat in the least defined corner of Chinese individual income tax. Resident individuals were taxable on worldwide income in principle, but no rule said when trust income arose, who filed, or how funding, growth, distributions, and termination should each be characterized. Enforcement was sporadic and varied by locality, and structures that retained income inside the trust could defer any reckoning indefinitely. Trustees held the records, valuations, and entity financials offshore, while Chinese filing deadlines assumed documents close at hand. That mismatch between offshore opacity and onshore deadlines is the pain point the new package attacks.

What Changed: Three Rules Covering the Trust Lifecycle

The Ministry of Finance and the State Taxation Administration closed the gap on 24 July 2026: they jointly issued Announcement No. 21 with the substantive treatment across 18 articles, and the STA the same day issued Announcement No. 15 with administration across 16 articles plus five forms, both effective immediately, supported by an official interpretation and press Q&A the same day. Three developments carry most of the weight.

First, funding the trust is now a taxable disposition. A resident individual's transfer into an offshore trust is taxed as property transfer income at 20 percent on the gain, meaning market value at transfer less original value and reasonable expenses, with basis stepped up to that market value. The funding definition looks through layers to whoever actually funds, bears, or controls the property, including through entities the trust holds or manages, and mixed resident and nonresident funding of one trust is treated as wholly resident. Parking appreciated assets offshore before sale no longer moves the gain out of reach.

Second, growth is taxed every year whether distributed or not. Income of a resident trust and its controlled overseas entities is attributed annually to the resident in two categories that cannot offset each other, with same-year gains and losses offsetting only within property transfer income, no loss carryforward, no deduction for trustee and advisory fees, and related-party transfers measured at market value with related-party losses ring-fenced (barred from offsetting other income). Amounts already taxed are not taxed again on distribution. The critical comparison is with the controlled foreign company rule, which requires showing no reasonable business need and a low foreign tax burden before attribution. Annual trust taxation needs no such preconditions. Retention inside the vehicle, the core of most deferral planning, has lost its function.

Third, every route of exit now carries tax consequences. Distributions to residents, liquidation gains at termination, exit from residence, and death each may trigger tax on market appreciation at 20 percent, with a 60-day deeming rule where liquidation drags, stub-year charges, and basis step-ups that prevent double counting. Loans left outstanding at year-end, expense payments, free use of trust property, and benefits routed through third parties or related parties are deemed distributions. Migration planning is directly addressed: foreign nationality or permanent residency does not end residence treatment where main economic interests stay in China. Genuine payment difficulty on termination or death earns five-year installments on timely filing, which is timing relief rather than forgiveness.

Control follows substance throughout. Coverage turns on directly or indirectly holding, in aggregate, 25 percent or more of the equity, voting rights, shares, income rights, or similar interests in an overseas entity, with multi-tier indirect holdings computed by multiplying the ratios at each tier and an intermediate tier holding more than 50 percent counted as 100 percent, substantive command over funding and operations, entity tests on passive income, staffing, personal use of funds, and local decision-making, and overseas income-tax-type credits remain available under the Individual Income Tax Law.

Transition: What the 90-Day Window Actually Means

Specified historic matters can be settled within 90 days of implementation without late surcharge. Counted in days with the starting day excluded, that period ends on 22 October 2026, rolling to the next day if the last day is a statutory holiday. In scope are resident funding transfers from 2023 through 2025, nonresident funding from 2023 through implementation with unpaid tax, and pre-2026 trust income and covered distributions packed without category distinction into interest, dividend, and bonus income. From 1 January 2026 the current rules apply in full, and afterwards the ordinary surcharge and evasion-penalty machinery returns.

A simplified illustration shows how the pieces fit (hypothetical with explicit assumptions: a resident individual funded an offshore trust in 2023 with shares then worth 500 million yuan against an original cost of 100 million; the trust earned 40 million yuan in dividends in each of 2024 and 2025, undistributed; no foreign tax paid). The 2023 funding gain of 400 million falls inside the regulation's look-back and is declarable by 22 October 2026 without surcharge as property transfer income at 20 percent. The 2024 and 2025 dividend income, 80 million in total, is packed without category distinction into interest, dividend, and bonus income for the same window. From 2026 onward the trust's yearly income is attributed annually in its proper categories whether distributed or not. Change any assumption — funding before 2023, distributed income, foreign tax paid — and the answer changes with it, which is why asset-by-asset reconstruction is the work.

Impact and What to Do

Taken together, the three highlights point in one direction: the offshore trust is no longer a vehicle for deferring tax, but a transparent arrangement that must be computed and reported year by year. The practical effect is a corresponding shift from tax efficiency to transparency, governance, and succession planning for every taxpayer with a China nexus. Trustees move from record-keepers to compliance counterparts, computing income by category and year and assisting with filing, while Chinese translations are required for foreign-language materials and pre-existing trusts must produce establishment year, the 2025 annual report, and historical financials at first filing. Resident filings fall between 1 March and 30 June, event filings by the 15th of the following month, and halls-first filing applies while electronic channels catch up.

Affected taxpayers should complete a systematic review before 22 October: inventory every trust and trust-like arrangement with settlors, beneficiaries, and controlling persons, map entities and assets against the control tests, reconcile market against original values asset by asset for 2023 through 2026, classify by announced category, and prepare the five forms with installment evaluation where relevant. Then build the durable process: confirmed competent authority under the three-tier test of related business registration, domestic property location, then habitual residence, foreign tax credit certificates in hand with attention to how credits stack across trust, entity, and beneficiary levels, trustee reporting packages that arrive before Chinese deadlines, and assigned ownership for the annual cycle and event reporting. Valuation standards for illiquid assets, credit stacking across levels, and reserved-powers treatment remain implementation-dependent and should be tracked as guidance emerges. In assessing their own exposure, taxpayers should keep three time dimensions apart: the 90-day no-surcharge filing window, the three-year look-back from 1 January 2023 that the regulation draws for establishment-stage matters, and the annual filing obligation that applies from 2026 onward.
