China Land Appreciation Tax Dispute Puts Spotlight on Equity-Transfer Structures

Tianjin real estate planning clash exposes weakness in LAT enforcement rules

Over the past 15 years, Chinese real estate assets have appreciated dramatically. In today’s market, any seller disposing of immovable property, such as land use rights or buildings, acquired a decade ago will likely realize substantial gains. These gains are subject not only to enterprise income tax but also to Land Appreciation Tax, a separate transaction-based levy imposed specifically on the disposal of real estate property in China.

Land Appreciation Tax and Its Impact

Land Appreciation Tax is assessed alongside income tax and can represent a major cost in real estate transactions. In large-scale deals, particularly those involving long-held or high-value assets, the Land Appreciation Tax liability alone can run into billions of U.S. dollars. This tax technically applies only to direct transfers of real estate and land use rights, it does not, on its face, apply to equity transactions.

To manage this tax exposure, some corporate sellers have structured transactions to convert real estate asset transfers into equity transfers, in an effort to fall outside the scope of the Land Appreciation Tax.

Common Transaction Structure

A widely observed planning structure involves three key steps:

· The seller first establishes a special-purpose entity.

· The seller contributes the Chinese real estate assets into this newly formed entity through a capital injection.

· Shortly thereafter, the seller transfers the equity interests in the special-purpose entity to the buyer.

Under this arrangement:

· In Step 2, the contribution of real estate into the special-purpose entity may trigger income tax, but not Land Appreciation Tax. Certain other transaction taxes (e.g., value-added tax or deed tax) may also be deferred.

· In Step 3, the equity sale should give rise to little or no income tax, as the seller’s tax basis would have been refreshed in Step 2. The seller would also take the position that no Land Appreciation Tax is due, since the asset being transferred is equity, not real estate.

The Enforcement Landscape: Mixed Practice

The primary uncertainty lies in Step 3, where the transfer of equity is used to achieve a tax result that would otherwise be unattainable in a direct sale.

In a notable 2000 case from Guangxi Province, a Chinese seller transferred equity in a company whose only material assets were Chinese real estate. When the seller argued that the transaction should not trigger Land Appreciation Tax, local authorities sought guidance from the central tax administration. The State Taxation Administration issued a formal notice (Notice No. 687), stating that the equity transfer should be looked through, and that Land Appreciation Tax should be imposed based on the underlying real estate.

Since that time, similar cases have been reported across various provinces. In general, local tax authorities have adopted a position consistent with Notice No. 687, issuing local-level notices and mandates calling on auditors to examine transactions that convert real estate asset deals into equity deals. However, enforcement has been inconsistent, in some cases, the transfer of a special-purpose entity holding real estate has proceeded without challenge on Land Appreciation Tax grounds.

Legal Controversy: Substance-Over-Form vs. Statutory Text

The tax authorities’ rationale is grounded in anti-avoidance principles. They argue that the three-step structure lacks economic substance and is primarily designed to avoid Land Appreciation Tax. Accordingly, they treat the steps as a tax avoidance scheme and disregard the corporate form.

While this reasoning may appear sound from a policy standpoint, it faces serious legal limitations.

Land Appreciation Tax in China is governed by the Interim Regulation on Land Appreciation Tax, which does not contain any provision authorizing a substance-over-form doctrine or a step-transaction analysis. In contrast, Chinese income tax regulations do contain provisions permitting the disregard of legal entities under specific anti-avoidance rules. However, those authorities cannot be extrapolated to apply to Land Appreciation Tax.

In effect, Notice No. 687 and similar local directives lack a clear legal basis, as they are not supported by the regulatory text of the Land Appreciation Tax regime. This exposes a major vulnerability in the current enforcement approach.

Outlook and Strategic Considerations

In light of the above, taxpayers who adopt this three-step planning structure, and are subsequently challenged by tax authorities, may have a strong legal basis to dispute any Land Appreciation Tax assessment in court. If the case is well-prepared and pursued through litigation, there is a material chance that the taxpayer would prevail, based on current law.

If the tax authorities wish to close this perceived loophole, the proper course would be to amend the Land Appreciation Tax regulation or implementing rules to explicitly address these transaction structures, either by targeting Step 2 as a deemed disposition or Step 3 as a reportable anti-avoidance transaction. Until such regulatory changes are made, the existing law appears to tilt in favor of taxpayers who are willing to assert their rights through legal proceedings.
