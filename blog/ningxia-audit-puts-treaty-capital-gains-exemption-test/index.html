Ningxia Audit Puts Treaty Capital Gains Exemption to the Test

Authorities scrutinize migration, ownership, and immovable property in UK–China case

Last month, a new audit case was reported in Ningxia, a provincial-level region in mid-western China, shedding light on how Chinese tax authorities continue to scrutinize treaty-based claims for capital gains exemption.

In this case, a non-resident enterprise (“NRE”) disposed of equity in a Chinese company and realized a substantial capital gain. The NRE sought to claim exemption from Chinese income tax under the capital gains article of the UK–China Income Tax Treaty (“UK–China Treaty”). However, the local tax authorities in Ningxia initiated an audit to evaluate the validity of that treaty position, setting the stage for yet another example of China’s evolving stance on treaty entitlement.

Case Background and Timeline

The NRE was established in Mauritius and acquired shares in a Chinese company (“target”) in 2015. The target was engaged in wind power generation. In July 2017, the target was listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange, and the NRE’s shareholding in the target was 14% at that time. In August 2018, the NRE moved its management and control from Mauritius to the UK (“Migration”).

In September 2018, the NRE began to transfer the shares of the target in the open market. As of April 2022, after more than 30 transfers, the NRE disposed of all its 14% equity in the target. For each transfer, the NRE took a position under Article 13 of the UK-China Treaty that no Chinese withholding tax on the gains should apply.

Under the UK-China Treaty and the Chinese regulations, all four tests below should be satisfied to qualify for the withholding tax exemption.

  • Tax Residency Test: The NRE was a UK tax resident at the time of the transfers.

  • Immovable Property Test: No more than 50% of the target’s assets, including direct and indirect ownership, consisted of Chinese immovable property at the time of the transfers and during the previous three years.

  • Ownership Test: The NRE’s (direct, indirect, and constructive) ownership in the target was less than 25% at the time of the transfers and during the previous 12 months.

  • Principal Purpose Test: The NRE did not undertake any arrangement that had a principal purpose of meeting one of the tests above.

Findings from the Ningxia Audit

The Ningxia tax authorities performed an in-depth review of the defense materials submitted, conducted on-site investigations, initiated information exchanges with foreign competent authorities, etc., along the lines of the four tests. The main results were as follows:

· Tax Residency Test and Principal Purpose Test

The NRE provided a tax residency certificate (“TRC”) issued by the HMRC to show that its UK tax residency commenced in August 2018. The tax auditors did not dispute the validity of the TRC. However, as the transfers of the target’s shares started almost right after the Migration, the tax authorities blended the Principal Purpose Test into the Tax Residency Test and questioned the reasons for the arrangement.

The NRE submitted documents to support the commercial considerations behind the Migration. The game-changer argument was the similarities between the UK-China Treaty and the income tax treaty between Mauritius and China (“Mauritius-China Treaty”) in Article 13. The two treaties shared comparable requirements for capital gains exemption. The NRE contended that it would have qualified for the exemption under the Mauritius-China Treaty without the Migration. In the end, the Chinese tax authorities accepted that treaty shopping was not a primary intention behind the Migration and acknowledged the UK tax residency of the NRE.

· Immovable Property Test

On a high level, meeting the Immovable Property Test means that no more than 50% of the assets owned by the target, directly and indirectly, consisted of Chinese immovable property. The testing period is the three years before each transfer.

The tax authorities examined the immovable assets of the target, including buildings, land use rights, construction-in-progress, etc., and considered their potential appreciation in value. They also investigated whether any fixed assets of the target had characteristics of immovable property. The auditors found that some of the target’s wind power generation equipment was buried underground and digging it up would cause significant damage to its value. They referenced the PRC Property Law and the Civil Code and categorized such underground equipment as “immovable property and accessories”. Nonetheless, even the broadened definition could not push the percentage of the target’s Chinese immovable property above the 50% threshold based on the detailed calculations. Therefore, the tax authorities admitted that the Immovable Property Test was met.

· Ownership Test

The NRE’s direct ownership in the target was less than 25% throughout the 12 months before the transfers. However, the Ningxia tax authorities also looked for any indirect and constructive ownership of the NRE in the target since the 25% threshold includes these forms of ownership as well. The auditors checked four major related parties of the NRE within the affiliated group to see if they had any shares in the target that could be attributed to the NRE. Additionally, the auditors looked into the top 10 direct shareholders of the target from 2015 to 2022 to see if any of them were related to the NRE, and whether their ownership in the target could be attributed to the NRE. During the process, the Chinese tax auditors used the information exchange mechanism to verify the submissions made by the NRE. After all the research, the tax auditors accepted that the NRE passed the Ownership Test, as no evidence justifying additional attribution was found.

The investigation concluded favorably for the taxpayer. The Ningxia tax authorities acknowledged that the NRE’s gains resulting from the 30-plus transfers of the target’s shares between 2018 and 2022 met the criteria for treaty exemption.

Key Takeaways for Multinationals

This case highlights the government’s emphasis on fact-gathering and verification in a Chinese treaty audit. As tax authorities are putting in significant efforts in this area, e.g., interviewing employees and counterparties, performing on-site due diligence, conducting database research, exchanging information with other jurisdictions, etc., it is prudent for taxpayers to assume that experienced examiners would likely have access to crucial facts during an audit.

The case reflects a positive shift towards a more legal-oriented approach in Chinese audits and defense. The government placed more weight on backing up its viewpoints with legal support. For instance, in this case, the auditors expanded the definition of immovable property citing the PRC Property Law and Civil Code, etc. In response, taxpayers should anticipate the potential interpretations of the government, perform rigorous legal analysis to develop counterarguments and substantiate them with evidence.

The case also shows that the tax authorities tend to scrutinize the Principal Purpose Test when a treaty claim is preceded by a restructuring. If the taxpayer’s treaty position is strengthened after the restructuring (e.g., migration from a non-treaty country to a treaty country, etc.), it is recommended to have solid supporting documents ready that can prove the predominant non-tax reasons behind the restructuring. Contemporaneous legal and business documents tend to carry more persuasive power than those prepared at the time of audit. A forward-looking approach could help overcome resistance from the tax authorities about the principal purpose during an audit.
