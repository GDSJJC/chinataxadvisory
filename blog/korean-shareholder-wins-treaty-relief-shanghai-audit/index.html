Korean Shareholder Wins Treaty Relief in Shanghai Audit

Safe harbor under Bulletin 9 helps MNC secure 5% WHT rate despite limited substance

A recent Chinese tax audit case illustrates how treaty qualification arguments rooted in “derivative benefits” logic, commonly seen in U.S. Limitation on Benefits (“LOB”) provisions, can prevail in a People’s Republic of China (“PRC”) audit. The case is notable not only for the favorable outcome but also for the persistence and technical sophistication of the multinational taxpayer’s defense.

This stands as one of the few publicly known examples where a reduced dividend withholding tax rate was successfully defended in China based on a multi-tiered foreign ownership structure.

Case Background: Dividend Distribution to a Korean Shareholder

The Chinese taxpayer in this case was a joint venture formed in 2011 (“China Co”). A South Korean company held a 45% equity interest as a minority shareholder. In 2016, China Co’s board of directors declared a RMB 200 million dividend, with RMB 90 million allocated to the Korean shareholder.

Under Article 10 of the China–South Korea income tax treaty, dividends paid to a Korean enterprise holding at least 25% equity in a Chinese company may qualify for a reduced 5% withholding tax rate, subject to satisfaction of several conditions. The Korean shareholder invoked the treaty and paid 5% withholding tax via remittance by China Co.

In 2017, the in-charge tax authority launched an audit of the treaty position.

Treaty Requirements: Three Core Tests

To qualify for the 5% reduced rate, the Korean shareholder needed to demonstrate that:

  • It was a tax resident of Korea.

  • It held at least 25% of China Co for the required period.

  • It was the “beneficial owner” of the dividend income.

The Korean shareholder passed the first two tests without dispute. The controversy centered on the third.

The Dispute: “Beneficial Ownership” Under Scrutiny

Chinese tax authorities have consistently taken the view that to be a beneficial owner of a China-sourced dividend, the recipient must carry out substantive business activities in its country of residence and maintain control over the dividend.

In this case, the Korean shareholder was a passive holding company, managing investments in 13 Chinese subsidiaries with a staff of only two directors and two secretaries. The tax auditors argued that its level of functional substance, risk-taking, and asset ownership was too low relative to the dividend received. Based on this, they challenged the taxpayer’s entitlement to the 5% treaty rate.

The Counterargument: Substance at the Ultimate Parent Level

Rather than accept the tax authority’s position, the Korean shareholder mounted a well-reasoned defense. It argued that:

  • The ultimate parent company was a Korean sovereign wealth investment organization wholly owned by the Korean government;

  • This parent company held 100% of the Korean shareholder through several wholly owned intermediate Korean entities; and

  • Each Korean subsidiary managed investments in different regions, and the Korean shareholder was designated to oversee Chinese assets.

The taxpayer emphasized that the ownership structure was commercially driven, not designed for treaty shopping, and that the dividend income remained within the Korean tax net at all times.

Turning Point: Safe Harbor Relief Under Bulletin 9

After prolonged negotiation, a regulatory breakthrough shifted the case. In Spring 2018, the Chinese central tax authorities issued Bulletin 9, which included a safe harbor provision directly applicable to multi-tier Korean investment structures.

The safe harbor allowed a shareholder to qualify as the beneficial owner, even with limited operating substance, if the following were met:

  • The ultimate Korean parent would have qualified as the beneficial owner if it held China Co directly;

  • The parent held 100% of the Korean shareholder, directly or indirectly;

  • All entities in the chain were tax residents of Korea.

Though Bulletin 9 applied prospectively from April 2018, the taxpayer submitted comprehensive supporting documents to demonstrate that the safe harbor criteria were fully met, including:

  • Tax residency certificates for all Korean entities;

  • Ownership charts;

  • Board resolutions and meeting records;

  • Dividend cash flow tracking;

  • Evidence of operational substance at the Korean parent level (e.g., over 1,400 employees managing more than USD 100 billion in diversified assets).

Case Outcome: Treaty Position Accepted

Despite the safe harbor not being retroactive, the local tax bureau accepted the taxpayer’s arguments and confirmed eligibility for the 5% treaty withholding rate. While the auditors were not technically bound to apply Bulletin 9 to the 2016 transaction, they appeared persuaded by:

  • The strength and consistency of the taxpayer’s legal and policy arguments;

  • The compelling level of economic substance at the parent level;

  • The safe harbor’s role as a forward-looking endorsement of the taxpayer’s position by the central tax authorities.

The audit concluded with no adjustment.

Takeaways for Multinational Taxpayers

First, the case shows that the safe harbor is real and effective. Though often dismissed as merely theoretical, the safe harbor provisions in Bulletin 9 can, in practice, be successfully invoked, even in contentious audits. This case serves as concrete proof that derivative-benefits-style reasoning can indeed operate within China’s treaty enforcement framework.

Second, substantive documentation proves decisive. The taxpayer’s success rested on its ability to articulate the commercial rationale of its structure and to demonstrate genuine substance, even if that substance did not lie at the immediate shareholder level. A strong treaty defense goes beyond legal argument; it demands well-organized, persuasive evidence.

Finally, persistence and thoughtful policy framing can alter the outcome. The Korean shareholder’s strategic patience, negotiating steadily while grounding its arguments in OECD principles and anti-abuse policy logic, ultimately persuaded the auditors. In China’s evolving enforcement landscape, thorough preparation and persistent, principled advocacy can still prevail.

Final Thought

In a regulatory landscape where beneficial ownership tests remain a central treaty risk, this case offers a rare but instructive precedent. It reminds multinational groups that success in treaty audits depends not only on structure, but on strategy, documentation, and clarity of purpose.
