Ongoing Tax Audit Trends and Key Lessons from a Southern China Case

Local authorities uncover hidden risks in outbound payments, TP, R&D, and IIT

Since the beginning of 2025, tax authorities across China have intensified audits of corporate taxpayers. These audits are typically not framed as formal investigations but often commence through targeted information requests, data analysis, and risk identification based on pre-determined focus areas. Many companies have received “tax risk reminder” notices prompting self-review and voluntary rectification. This approach enables authorities to engage a broader range of taxpayers more efficiently while achieving revenue goals without escalating compliance tensions.

Recent cases suggest that these inspections frequently focus on areas often overlooked by companies during daily tax management. These issues are generally not the result of deliberate evasion but rather stem from negligence or misunderstanding of complex compliance obligations. A recent case in Southern China offers instructive lessons across several common areas of risk.

Tax Audit Findings

  • Outbound Payment

The audit, initiated toward the end of 2023, was triggered by the tax authority’s enhanced access to outbound payment data from taxpayers. Under Chinese regulations, when a taxpayer makes a non-trade payment to an overseas party, it must complete a tax recordal to inform authorities. While these transactions are not always reviewed immediately, the authorities frequently revisit them to ensure proper tax compliance, and also to meet tax revenue collection targets.

In this case, during the summer of 2023, the taxpayer made a payment to its European affiliate but failed to withhold both Value-Added Tax (“VAT”) and income tax. Under Chinese rules, royalties related to technology transfers are subject to 6% VAT unless the taxpayer has proactively secured a certification from the local Science and Technology Bureau. Additionally, a 10% withholding income tax generally applies based on the payer’s tax residency. Due to a misunderstanding of these requirements, the taxpayer neither withheld the necessary taxes nor obtained the appropriate certification for VAT exemption. Consequently, the taxpayer was required to pay the under-withheld VAT and income tax, along with penalty interest.

While the initial audit trigger was the taxpayer’s outbound payment and failure to withhold taxes, once attention was drawn, the tax authority expanded the inspection and uncovered three additional areas where tax adjustments were imposed.

  • Intercompany Lending

The audit also revealed issues with domestic intercompany loans. The taxpayer, an affiliate of a foreign-headquartered multinational, had extended excess cash to its sister companies in China through intercompany payables, which remained outstanding for extended periods without charging interest. Although Chinese transfer pricing (“TP”) regulations primarily focus on cross-border transactions, domestic dealings are also subject to scrutiny when there are income tax rate differentials between related parties. In this instance, the taxpayer’s tax rate was higher than that of its borrowing affiliate, resulting in potential tax base erosion. As a result, the tax bureau imposed TP adjustments for 2021 and 2022, including additional income tax and 6% VAT on the deemed interest income, since interest income is VAT taxable under Chinese law.

  • Disallowed R&D Super Deduction

Chinese tax rules allow a bonus deduction for qualifying R&D expenditures, but certain criteria must be satisfied. First, the R&D activities must involve genuine innovation and uncertainty of outcome. Simple modifications to existing processes without the potential for meaningful technological advancement generally do not qualify. Second, if R&D activities are outsourced, the taxpayer must properly register the project with the appropriate government authority to claim the deduction on 80% of the outsourced costs.

In this case, the taxpayer’s activities were found lacking in sufficient innovation and technological uncertainty. Moreover, the taxpayer failed to perform the required registration for contract R&D projects and did not maintain detailed documentation of the expenditures. As a result, the tax authority disallowed the claimed R&D super deduction, leading to additional income tax liability.

  • Employee compensation and IIT non-compliance

The audit also uncovered non-compliance related to individual income tax (“IIT”) withholding for employee compensation. First, the taxpayer accrued salary expenses at year-end but failed to pay them by May 31 of the following year, the deadline for filing the enterprise income tax return. Under Chinese regulations, unpaid salaries can be deductible only if paid before the return filing deadline. As the payments were delayed beyond this period, the deduction was denied.

Additionally, the taxpayer had distributed gift cards to employees as a form of welfare and claimed the associated expenses for EIT purposes. However, under Chinese IIT rules, unless specific conditions are met, gift cards are considered taxable salary income and are subject to withholding. The taxpayer failed to withhold IIT on these distributions, creating significant compliance issues.

To address the under-withholding, the taxpayer needed to coordinate with current employees to recover the taxes from future salaries and absorb the tax liability for former employees, along with facing potential penalties. This case illustrates the importance of careful handling of non-cash employee benefits to avoid significant tax risks and administrative burdens.

Conclusion: early detection is key

This audit highlights the increasing scrutiny by Chinese tax authorities on a wide range of compliance matters, from outbound payments and TP to R&D incentives and employee compensation practices. Taxpayers should enhance their internal controls, maintain thorough documentation, and stay updated on the evolving regulatory landscape to mitigate tax risks and avoid costly penalties. Early identification and proactive management of potential issues are essential in today’s challenging tax environment.
