Shenzhen Audit Recasts Offshore Deal as Direct Transfer

Lack of substance at BVI and HK levels triggers PRC EIT liability

There is a new tax audit case in Shenzhen involving the indirect transfer of a Chinese company by a foreign entity. This transfer was recharacterized by the auditor as a direct transfer and became subject to Chinese Enterprise Income Tax (“EIT”). The case shows that the trend of monitoring offshore indirect transfers continues, and the local Chinese tax authorities are diligent in identifying targets and pushing for adjustment during audits. It is relevant for both the buyer and the seller in a M&A transaction involving the indirect transfer of Chinese operation to be aware of this implication and consider planning measures for risk mitigation.

Case Summary

In April 2023, the Futian District Tax Bureau in Shenzhen noticed, while sifting through disclosures from the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, that a foreign company (“transferor” or “taxpayer”) had transferred the shares of its offshore subsidiary, which indirectly resulted in the transfer of a Chinese company. The transferor entered into a share purchase agreement with an unrelated entity listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange (“acquirer”), involving the sale of the shares in a BVI company and certain debt for a total consideration of approximately USD 305 million. The BVI company’s sole asset was 100% ownership of a Hong Kong company, which in turn owned a Chinese company.

The tax authorities launched an audit and, at the end of the investigation, reclassified the offshore transaction as a direct transfer of the Chinese company. The foreign transferor was assessed approximately USD 23 million in Chinese EIT on the gains.

Commentary

Chinese tax law allows the authorities to recharacterize an indirect share transfer as a direct transfer of Chinese resident enterprise if the transaction is deemed, in their view, to lack a reasonable commercial purpose and is primarily aimed at avoiding Chinese EIT. In this case, although the BVI company did not engage in substantive business activities, the Hong Kong company did conduct local operations, including trading and brand promotion, leasing a storefront, and employing staff. However, the business operations mainly began in the fourth quarter of 2022, three months before the share purchase agreement was signed in early 2023.

The tax authorities concluded that both the BVI company and the Hong Kong company lacked substantial economic activities at the time of the share transfer. The taxpayer argued that the transaction’s purpose was to achieve strategic integration and collaboration between the corporate groups of the transferor and the acquirer, not to avoid Chinese EIT. However, the authorities rejected this argument, citing the lack of significant commercial activities by the BVI and the Hong Kong companies involved, and treated the transaction as a direct transfer of the Chinese company.

This case reaffirms the position of Chinese tax authorities that, when determining whether an offshore transfer has a reasonable commercial purpose, they will rely on quantitative criteria such as asset scale and composition, revenues and profits, and headcounts, rather than a narrative of non-tax reasons presented by the taxpayer.

The case also clarifies that, when determining whether an offshore holding company has sufficient active business to avoid Chinese tax liability, the economic substance should exist at the time of the transaction and in the period preceding the transaction. Therefore, if a foreign transferor intends to rely on the economic substance of intermediate holding companies to argue against the imposition of Chinese EIT, they need to ensure that such substance exists for at least a year before the transaction.
