Shenzhen Denies S Corp Treaty Claim on Capital Gains

Pass-through entity status blocks U.S. taxpayer from China–U.S. treaty relief

A recent case in China highlights the treaty qualification challenges faced by U.S. S Corporations in cross-border transactions. The case involved a denial of treaty relief on capital gains realized by a U.S. shareholder from the sale of Chinese listed shares, specifically due to the entity’s status as an S Corporation under U.S. tax law.

Case Background

The Chinese company (”China Sub”) was listed in the Shenzhen stock exchange in the PRC. One of its direct shareholders was a US company (”US Co”), which owned 16.5% in China Sub. US Co was, in turn, owned by a US individual shareholder.

In 2018, US Co transferred its 16.5% shares through the Shenzhen stock exchange for cash and recognized China-sourced capital gains from the sale.

Gains realized by a non-resident enterprise from transferring Chinese assets that are not effectively connected to a Chinese trade or business are normally subject to a 10% Chinese enterprise income tax, collected on a withholding basis.

Under Article 12 of the US-China income tax treaty, if a US taxpayer is a less-than-25% shareholder and realizes gains from transferring shares in a Chinese company, the gains could be exempt from the 10% Chinese withholding tax, suppose that the US taxpayer is a US tax resident and the Chinese company is not “land-rich.”

China Sub’s primary assets were not real estate in the PRC and therefore was not “land-rich.” In addition, US Co obtained a US tax residency certificate from the IRS. Pursuant to the China-US income tax treaty, US Co applied to the Chinese tax authorities for a refund of the 10% Chinese income tax that was withheld when the China Sub remitted the sales proceeds to US Co.

Regulatory Challenge

Upon reviewing the case, the Chinese tax authorities determined that US Co was structured as an S Corporation under the U.S. Internal Revenue Code and treated as a pass-through entity for U.S. federal income tax purposes. Importantly, while US Co submitted a U.S. tax residency certificate in support of its treaty claim, the certificate ultimately substantiated the residency of its individual U.S. shareholder, not of US Co itself. As a result, the authorities concluded that US Co did not qualify as a U.S. tax resident and was therefore ineligible for benefits under the China–U.S. income tax treaty.

This raised the question of whether US Co could be looked through for Chinese tax purposes, allowing the individual shareholder, as the ultimate taxpayer, to invoke the treaty. The Chinese tax authorities rejected this approach, stating that the China–U.S. treaty does not include a “look-through” provision permitting pass-through entities (e.g., partnerships or S Corporations) to be disregarded in the determination of treaty qualification. Under this interpretation, the transferor was US Co, not its individual owner, and US Co’s lack of treaty eligibility stood.

Consequently, the application for a withholding tax refund was denied.

Based on this reasoning, the outcome would likely be similar for other U.S. pass-through entities, such as LLCs electing disregarded entity (“DRE”) treatment under U.S. tax law. U.S. multinational groups that utilize S Corporations, partnerships, or DREs to hold investments in Chinese entities should carefully assess treaty qualification risks when planning an exit strategy. Entity classification under U.S. law does not automatically translate into treaty eligibility from the Chinese tax perspective.
