Downward Transfer Pricing Adjustments in China: Why It Deserves Extra Care

Refund barriers, forex hurdles, and customs interplay demand early planning

As the preparation and audit of 2024 financial statements draws to a close, multinational enterprises (“MNEs”) with operations in China should have already assessed the financial performance of their Chinese entities against established group transfer pricing (“TP”) policies. Now is the time to determine whether year-end or post-year-end TP adjustments are warranted for 2024, for instance, to align reported results with the group TP policy. These adjustments may involve upward corrections to raise profit margins or downward adjustments to reduce margins that exceed acceptable levels. While upward adjustments can present difficulties, downward adjustments come with their own distinct regulatory and practical challenges.

For instance, when a Chinese subsidiary of an MNE imports goods from a foreign affiliate for resale under a routine profit margin, it typically monitors its profitability throughout the year, making incremental fine-tuning to remain within an arm’s length range. However, major deviations may surface late in the year, leaving too much profit in China and too little time to respond. Downward adjustments serve to address this situation by remitting excess profits from China to overseas. By nature, they tend to attract closer scrutiny from tax and foreign exchange (“forex”) authorities, and a lack of preparation can lead to tax costs, regulatory concerns, and cash inefficiency for an MNE. That said, with thoughtful approach, they can be implemented.

External evidence for tax deduction

Downward adjustments are recorded as expenses with corresponding accounts payable, making them potentially tax-deductible. However, deductions call for robust supporting records. While intercompany agreements, commercial invoices, and TP reports are essential, the tax authorities also need to see external evidence for the expenses.

For downward adjustments involving imported goods, customs authorities expect to collect additional customs duties and import VAT because of the upward revision in importation prices. The Chinese subsidiary may voluntarily settle such customs duties and import VAT and use the customs payment records as external evidence for the tax deduction. This can mitigate customs compliance and tax deduction risks for the downward adjustments.

Collaborating with banks for remittance

Downward adjustments create payables that must be settled through remittance. While there’s no strict deadline for remittance, delays can invite additional tax scrutiny. If payables remain unsettled for extended periods, tax authorities may challenge their validity, and in some cases, they could be treated as cancelled debt for tax purposes and generate taxable income for the Chinese subsidiary.

China’s forex regulations limit options such as set offs to settle the payables, and converting payables into registered capital of the Chinese subsidiary is normally unfeasible without proper forex registration. These constraints make remittance crucial for preserving the tax deduction in the long term.

Banks play a key role in enforcing Chinese forex rules and ensuring cross-border payments align with regulatory parameters. To process remittances, they want detailed proof from the Chinese subsidiary, making them important partners in implementing the adjustments.

While the forex authority issued a Q&A guideline in 2021 regarding TP adjustments, its focus was on adjustments resulting from tax audits. This leaves room for variability in how banks handle self-initiated adjustments, underscoring the importance of communication with banks.

Tax or Customs documents as support

Internal documents like intercompany agreements, invoices, and TP reports can be provided by the MNE and its Chinese subsidiary with no difficulty. However, banks may ask for external support from tax or customs authorities.

Obtaining written tax endorsement for downward adjustments is not easy. Tax authorities are less inclined to approve adjustments that reduce Chinese taxable income, and tax rulings or audit notices supporting such TP adjustments are rare in practice.

For recurring or material downward adjustments, an Advance Pricing Agreement (“APA”) can provide a reliable framework for implementation. APAs predefine profit margins and pricing mechanisms, reducing TP risks and serving as government-recognized tax evidence for banks.

While APAs involve significant time and resources, i.e., 1-2 years for unilateral APAs and 2-3 years or more for bilateral ones, they’re a valuable tool for MNEs managing frequent or substantial adjustments in the long term.

For a Chinese subsidiary that is not covered by an APA and cannot obtain official tax records to meet the bank’s requirements, seeking evidence from the customs authority can be a viable alternative.

Customs authorities are generally receptive to downward TP adjustments that result in additional customs duties and import VAT, such as when intercompany transactions involve imported goods. However, obtaining updated customs declarations can be tricky, as customs authorities sometimes consider the original customs declarations final despite the extra collection of customs duties and import VAT. This creates a potential disconnect, as banks may demand updated customs declarations reflecting the downward adjustments for remittance.

To address this, taxpayers should start by clarifying the bank’s specific needs. Armed with this understanding, they can then communicate with customs authorities via established programs such as price negotiation or voluntary self-disclosure, to explore options and bridge the gap between customs practices and bank expectations.

A Cash repatriation tool

Downward adjustments aren’t just about TP. They can also be a valuable tool for cash repatriation. When the Chinese subsidiary faces cash trap issues in China, traditional repatriation options like dividends are constrained by its earnings limits. Intercompany service fees may carry significant tax and TP risks. Cross-border cash pooling arrangements and outbound loans only provide temporary solution to cash trap problems.

In contrast, downward adjustments may provide a permanent and defensible solution for repatriating cash, as its form and substance are well aligned. However, navigating the procedural complexities as described is necessary for execution.

Looking forward

Downward transfer pricing adjustments in China involve intricate regulatory landscapes, from forex constraints to tax and customs rules. Compared to upward adjustments, they warrant greater attention to preparation and planning, as they tend to face closer scrutiny from authorities.

Nonetheless, these challenges can be overcome with planning, alignment across stakeholders, and tools like APAs. By coordinating among the different external parties and assembling the necessary package, MNEs can successfully manage downward adjustments, mitigate compliance risks, and optimize cash utilization.
