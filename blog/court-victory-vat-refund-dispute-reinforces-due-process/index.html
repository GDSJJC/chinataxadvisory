Court Victory on VAT Refund Dispute Reinforces Due Process

Higher Court overturns $4M clawback, stressing taxpayer rights

This China Tax Update highlights a recent case where a taxpayer and the tax authorities couldn’t settle a VAT dispute, which ended up in court. The court of final appeal used the principle of due process to rule in favor of the taxpayer, overturning the authorities’ decision to reclaim about $4 million in VAT refunds. This ruling offers valuable guidance for handling tax disputes in China, both for taxpayers and tax authorities.

Case Background

The taxpayer, a Chinese company based in Ningbo, Zhejiang Province, engaged in export business between 2010 and 2012, purchasing high-fidelity audio cables from a domestic vendor and exporting them to Hong Kong. The taxpayer applied for export tax refunds on the input VAT paid for these cables, totaling around $4 million. In 2014, the Ningbo State Taxation Bureau’s Inspection Branch (”Field Examiner”) audited the taxpayer and reclassified its business in the export transaction as an agency rather than a “buy-sale” distribution, disqualifying it from the VAT refund.

The taxpayer sought an administrative appeal with the Ningbo State Taxation Bureau to challenge the decision but was unsuccessful. In 2017, the taxpayer filed a lawsuit with the Jiangbei District Court of Ningbo. The court found that the Field Examiner had not conducted a hearing on a matter significantly impacting the taxpayer’s interests and had not provided adequate opportunities for the taxpayer to present and defend its case, thus violating the principle of due process. In March 2018, the court overturned the tax authorities’ decision to reclaim the VAT refund.

The tax authorities appealed to the Ningbo Intermediate Court and won. However, the taxpayer disagreed and filed another appeal with the Zhejiang Provincial Higher Court (”Higher Court”).

The Higher Court determined that administrative bodies must notify affected parties and hear their statements and defenses to protect their legal rights before making adverse decisions. The Field Examiner’s decision to reclaim the $4 million VAT refund significantly impacted the taxpayer’s business operations but did not provide sufficient opportunities for the taxpayer to defend its case. The Higher Court agreed with the District Court that the Field Examiner had not complied with the due process principle. In June 2020, the Higher Court issued a final ruling in favor of the taxpayer and ordered the tax authorities to return the clawed-back VAT refund.

Commentary and Implications

In recent years, with the development of China’s judicial system, more Chinese taxpayers become assertive in defending their rights during tax audits. This case is a prime example.

The highlight of this case is the application of the principle of due process. The key technical issue was whether the taxpayer purchased the goods for export from the vendor or merely acted as an export agent, as the former would qualify it for a VAT refund while the latter would not. Although the taxpayer provided ample evidence that it bore substantial risks for the export sales, thereby owning the exported goods in substance, the courts focused on the procedural aspects of how the case was handled. They examined whether the Field Examiner adhered to the principle of due process and whether merely following statutory procedures was sufficient for procedural legality.

The PRC Tax Collection and Administration Law, its implementation rules, and procedural regulations do not specify a hearing process for tax matters unless penalties are involved. In this case, while the tax bureau did not violate any mandatory statutory provisions, the Higher Court emphasized the Field Examiner’s failure to adequately protect the taxpayer’s rights to make statements and present defense as the key basis for the ruling.

This case highlights the increasing significance of due process in China’s tax judicial practice. The taxpayer likely presented defense arguments during the field examination. However, the Higher Court found that there was insufficient formal evidence showing that the tax authorities properly considered these arguments and communicated their findings before making their adjustment decision. This communication is crucial for the taxpayer to understand the tax authorities’ position, reasons, and legal basis, as well as to provide additional proof for rebuttal. Without this process, the taxpayer’s right to effectively defend itself could be compromised.
