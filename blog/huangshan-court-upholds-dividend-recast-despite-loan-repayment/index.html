Huangshan Court Upholds Dividend Recast Despite Loan Repayment

Shareholder loans retroactively taxed as dividends under Caishui 158

A recently reported tax case from Huangshan, Anhui Province sheds light on how Chinese tax authorities may recast shareholder loans as deemed dividends, even if those loans have already been repaid by the time of audit. The case illustrates the authorities’ and courts’ strict, literal interpretation of existing regulations, highlighting the risk that loan arrangements between Chinese companies and individual shareholders could trigger unexpected individual income tax (“IIT”) liabilities.

Case Background

The taxpayer was a real estate development company structured as a Chinese limited liability company with three individual shareholders. Toward the end of 2010, the company extended loans to its shareholders, which were subsequently repaid in full by May 2012.

On February 28, 2013, the local tax authorities conducted an audit against the company. During the investigation, the tax auditors recast the loans as dividends to the individual shareholders. Under the Chinese tax law, when a company pays a dividend to an individual shareholder, it should withhold the Chinese withholding tax on the payment. As a result, the tax authorities determined that the Chinese company under-withheld individual income tax on the deemed dividend and issued a tax adjustment notice.

The Chinese enterprise disagreed with the assessment and filed an administrative appeal. The field examiners’ decision was upheld by the upper-level reviewing tax authorities. The taxpayer filed lawsuits at the district and the municipal courts at the tax authorities but lost them.

Regulatory Interpretation

Under Caishui [2003] No. 158 (Caishui 158), when a Chinese shareholder borrows money from a company that it invests in, any outstanding balance as of the year-end “may” be deemed as a dividend to the shareholder, to the extent that the shareholder loan was not used by the individual shareholder for the business operation of the invested company. The provision was implemented to counter a prevailing practice that Chinese individual shareholders often obtain cash from their investee companies for personal use over an extended period without repaying but do not call it dividend to defer Chinese individual income tax.

In the case above, the tax authorities and the courts argue that the two essential elements of Caishui 158 were all satisfied for a dividend characterization. Specifically,

  • The shareholder loan was not returned as of the year-end of 2010 or 2011.

  • The shareholder loan was not used for the business operation of the Chinese enterprise.

Although the tax authorities’ decision and the rulings of the courts reflect the literal requirements of the aforementioned tax regulations, the case is controversial in that the purpose of the regulation is to target the situations where the parties intend to distribute a dividend but disguise it as a shareholder loan.

In the case above, by the time the audit was launched, the shareholder loans were fully repaid. Even though the loans straddled the tax year ends of 2010 and 2011, at the time of the audit, the tax investigators already had the benefit of hindsight to know that they were true “loans” rather than “dividends.”

Caishui 158 does not require the tax authorities to recast loans as dividends in all cases that meet the two criteria above. The regulation uses the word “may” rather than “should.” A more reasonable approach would have been to treat a repaid loan as a true loan rather than applying Caishui 158 mechanically to derive an artificial result that deviates from reality. On the flip side, the taxpayer in the case could have mounted a more effective defense during the appeal and the court proceedings.

Practical Takeaways

This case serves as a cautionary reminder for taxpayers to closely observe the requirements set out in Caishui 158. When companies extend loans to individual shareholders, particularly for personal use, it is critical to ensure that such loans are fully repaid by year-end. Failure to do so may lead to the loans being recharacterized as dividends, triggering individual income tax (“IIT”) obligations.

More importantly, businesses in this situation risk being penalized for under-withholding, with potential penalties reaching up to 300% of the tax amount due. Given the strict enforcement approach observed in this case, companies should treat shareholder loans with caution and incorporate appropriate year-end settlement planning into their compliance processes.
