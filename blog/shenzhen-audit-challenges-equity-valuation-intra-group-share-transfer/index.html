Shenzhen Audit Challenges Equity Valuation in Intra-Group Share Transfer

Missed filing deadline and RMB 100M upward adjustment highlight compliance risks

In this edition of the China Tax Update, we spotlight a recent tax audit case in Shenzhen that carries important implications for multinational groups engaging in intra-group share transfers within China.

The case centers on how fair market value (“FMV”) is determined for equity interest transfers between related parties. Tax authorities in Shenzhen challenged the valuation methodology adopted by the transferring shareholders, ultimately making an upward adjustment of over RMB 100 million to the declared transaction value. This led to an additional corporate income tax assessment of approximately RMB 30 million, along with related late payment penalties.

Case Background

The audit case relates to the transfers of a Chinese company in the Qianhai district of Shenzhen that occurred during the pandemic. In 2021, certain equity interests of a Chinese company (“Target”) in the Qianhai district of Shenzhen were sold for RMB 44 million in cash among multiple related party shareholders. Target’s main asset was a shopping center in Shenzhen. The transaction was closed successfully, with the requisite government registration performed and the tax filings made. The parties submitted a valuation report prepared by a third-party appraiser (“Appraiser”) to substantiate that the amount paid in the transaction represented fair market value (“FMV”).

In July 2022, the local district tax bureau audited the transaction. The tax examiners challenged that Target was severely undervalued, citing that the Shenzhen municipal government’s real estate transaction center (“RETC”) gave a higher appraisal value to the shopping center based on similar commercial properties.

The tax audit team interviewed the primary selling shareholder (“transferor”) and asked it to elaborate on the mechanism of the valuation. The transferor explained that Target’s revenue was mainly derived from leasing out the shopping center to tenants, and a Discounted Cash Flow (“DCF”) method was used to estimate its value. Due to the impact of the epidemic, the rental revenues of the shopping center were significantly reduced in 2021, and the prospect of recovery was uncertain at the time of the transaction. Therefore, a lower forecast of future rents was adopted in the DCF. In addition, the transactional parties expected that the shopping center would need large-scale renovation when the pre-existing leases expired, and this would require material cash outlays. These two factors contributed to lower valuation from the DCF. The transferor further argued that the appraised value given by the RETC was inadequate because it was based on the economic environment several years ago and did not fully consider the impact of Covid-19.

The tax audit team believed that the transaction value was well below the normal value of a similar property in Shenzhen, and the reasons cited by the transferor were insufficient to fully account for the shortfall. They interviewed the third-party appraiser who issued the valuation report for the transaction and obtained further details on the application of the DCF method. They then formulated more detailed challenges on the parameters and assumptions used in the DCF.

After consultation and communication and soliciting the opinions of a team of lawyers, the tax authorities and the transactional parties agreed to use a mutually accepted appraisal company to re-perform the valuation. The re-issued valuation report increased the FMV of Target from RMB 44 million to RMB 201 million. As a result, the tax authorities issued a deficiency notice to the taxpayers in the transaction and collected an additional tax and interest.

Key Takeaways for Taxpayers

This case reminds MNCs of the importance of accurate and compliant valuation practices during intercompany transfers. It underscores the need for businesses to thoroughly evaluate and document their valuation methodologies to ensure they align with Chinese tax regulations. Specifically, the following points are worth noting.

The transferor here used Covid-19 as a defense and in effect argued that at the time of the transaction, it could not have foreseen the length and the ultimate impact of the pandemic on its business and had to apply conservative assumptions. The result of the case illustrates that the tax authorities are not willing to embrace the “no-hindsight” argument readily and may challenge it if the taxpayer cannot produce compelling evidence to support it.

While the tax authorities have been focusing on the income method (e.g., DCF) of valuation for equity transactions, they have not given up on the asset method. Instead, they often expect both methods and use them to corroborate each other. This is particularly true when the target company has certain “hot” assets on its balance sheet such as valuable intellectual properties or real estate. In those situations, it is prudent to understand the impact of both methods during the planning stage.

Finally, although using a third-party appraisal report can reduce the chance of audit challenges, it is not bulletproof. The report needs to be technically robust. For instance, the income method of valuation is often sensitive to key assumptions. The transaction parties need to develop solid support to back them up. Otherwise, the tax auditors can set aside the valuation study submitted and request a re-evaluation by a re-assigned appraiser.
