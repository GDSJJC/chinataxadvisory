Shenzhen Audit Recasts Litigation Settlement into Royalty

Outbound payment recharacterized, triggering WHT and late interest

A recent audit case highlighting the potential risks associated with tax re-characterization of outbound payment was reported in Shenzhen, Southern China. In this case, the Nanshan District Tax Bureau (“NDTB”) in Shenzhen audited an outbound payment made to settle a lawsuit and concluded that the payment should be classified as royalty. As a result, a withholding tax was imposed, and late payment interest was assessed on the transaction.

Case Summary

In May 2023, the NDTB, when reviewing the tax recordals for outbound payments filed by entities in its local district, noted that a Chinese company (“Payor”) had just paid a significant fee to an unrelated foreign company (“Payee”) to settle a legal dispute, and claimed that no withholding tax should be due.

The tax officials interviewed the personnel of the Payor and gathered the following facts on the payment.

The legal dispute concerned six technical researchers of the payor. Before joining the R&D department of the payor, they were employed by a Chinese subsidiary of the payee. The payee argued that these six employees had contact with its product know-how and knew the related trade secrets during their employment with its subsidiary, and for that reason, they signed a non-compete agreement with its subsidiary to ensure technical confidentiality. Right after their resignation, they were hired by the payor, which operated in the same industry as the payee’s subsidiary. The payee believed that the payor recruited these personnel to gain access to its commercial secrets and proprietary technology. Hence, the payee filed a lawsuit against the payor. The two companies negotiated and agreed to settle the case out of court. Under the settlement agreement reached, the payor made a payment to the payee, which became the target of the tax examination above.

The NDTB believed that the settlement payment was in essence compensation for the payor’s infringement of the payee’s IP. The payor argued that the lawsuit was about its six researchers violating the non-compete agreement rather than infringing on the payee’s trade secrets or know-how. It explained that it wanted to settle because it planned to go public, and wished to avoid any lawsuits that could affect the IPO process. It disagreed with the characterization that the settlement payment was damages for IP infringement.

The tax officer counter-argued that based on the settlement agreement and various legal correspondences in the lawsuit, the payee sued because it believed that the payor breached its IP rights by hiring its technical personnel despite the non-compete agreement. The essence of the case was about IP rights, so the true nature of the out-of-court settlement was a royalty to compensate for the payor’s unauthorized use of the payee’s proprietary technology.

In the end, the NDTB assessed withholding tax and late payment interest on the payor, as the withholding agent, based on the royalty classification.

Technical Analysis

The case is about the proper characterization of a litigation settlement payment for Chinese income tax purposes. In the context of this case, if a settlement payment is classified as a royalty, it becomes subject to a Chinese withholding tax rate (normally 10%). However, if it is not considered as such, the tax treatment depends on whether the payment is associated with any service activities performed by the payee within China.

The payor’s stance was that, based on the form of the transaction, specifically referring to the payment not being classified as a royalty and not being associated with any service activity of the payee, there should be no Chinese withholding tax. This argument was primarily grounded in interpreting the settlement agreement’s literal language. On the other hand, the NDTB’s position had its merits as it emphasized a crucial aspect of the case. The core issue revolved around suspicions that the payor obtained unauthorized access to the payee’s IP by hiring the researchers. The NDTB found support for this interpretation in the court documents filed by the payee and the legal correspondence in the lawsuit.

From a procedural standpoint, when invoking a substance-over-form doctrine as the key argument in a tax audit, the NDTB should in principle initiate the anti-avoidance process prescribed in the Chinese GAAR regulations (STA Order 32). The establishment of an anti-avoidance case should be reported to the STA (the central tax authorities) and the conclusion of the case should be approved by the STA. The communications and notices to the taxpayer should follow the GAAR regulations. It is unclear whether the NDTB observed these procedures in the case.

Key Takeaways

According to the prevailing rules, when a Chinese taxpayer makes an overseas payment that is not capital in nature and does not pertain to tangible assets, it is generally required to file a tax recordal with the respective tax authority in charge. The taxpayer must also self-declare their tax position concerning this payment. The purpose of the tax recordal is to enable the tax authority to monitor such payments and potentially launch an investigation. When the taxpayer takes a position of no or reduced Chinese taxation under the domestic tax rules or treaties on a sizable outbound, this would usually attract the attention of the tax authorities.

Considering potential scrutiny by tax authorities, the defense must be grounded in a rigorous legal analysis and supported by relevant documents. Relying largely on the probability of discovery and challenge should not be the primary basis for a defense strategy. By maintaining meticulous records and ensuring they align with both legal requirements and the underlying nature of the transactions, the taxpayer will be better equipped to defend its position if necessary.

In cases where a risk of tax recast is identified, it is advisable to include a provision in the settlement agreement that specifies which party shall bear the cost of any withholding tax imposed. In the absence of prior alignment, the allocation of the withholding tax burden due to the audit adjustment may give rise to additional controversy among the parties.
