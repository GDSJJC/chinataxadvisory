Wuxi Audit Recasts HK Sub as Limited-Risk Operator in Semiconductor Probe

China imposes cost-plus markup, reallocates global profits to domestic IC design hub

A recent transfer pricing audit case reported in Wuxi, Jiangsu province, sheds light on China’s evolving approach to cross-border value chain arrangements, particularly in the semiconductor sector.

Background of the Taxpayer and Supply Chain Model

The taxpayer was a medium-sized Chinese company headquartered in the city of Wuxi. It was granted the High-and-New Technology Enterprise (”HNTE”) status and was primarily engaged in the R&D and design of integrated circuits (”IC”) in the radio frequency area. It operated under the fabless model, sourcing silicon wafers from unrelated foundries in Taiwan using its own designs, and selling the wafers to a wholly owned subsidiary in Hong Kong (“HK Sub”). HK Sub contracted with unrelated back-end manufacturers to perform assembly and testing on the wafers and sold the assembled chips to 3rd party customers outside the PRC.

Dispute over Functions, Assets, and Risks of HK Sub

This intercompany sale of wafers from the taxpayer to HK Sub in 2014 and 2015 was challenged by the Wuxi tax authorities. The HK Sub had only a few employees, and explained that its primary business activities were carried out by these personnel while they travelled in the PRC. HK Sub made offshore claim in Hong Kong and paid no local profits tax. The Wuxi tax authorities argued that HK Sub’s level of commercial substance could not justify its profitability, and sought to increase the selling price in the intercompany transaction.

The taxpayer counter-argued that HK Sub made substantial contribution in the supply chain because: (1) it possessed valuable “vendor pre-qualification” status for a number of key customers such as Samsung, which it believed to constitute important intangible assets; (2) it managed the assembly and testing activities performed by the 3rd party contract manufacturers; and (3) it bore other significant risks, such as financing risk, bad debt risk, inventory risk, and market risk, etc.

Final Adjustment: Markup Selection and Settlement Terms

Eventually the Wuxi tax authorities characterized HK Sub as akin to a semiconductor back-end manufacturer and used TNMM to develop an arm’s length range of profitability for it. In the government communications to the taxpayer, the Wuxi tax authorities selected a target Markup on Full Cost of 5% for HK Sub, assigning any excess profits of HK Sub during the audit period to the taxpayer. The taxpayer settled the case, making a self-adjustment of approximately US$ 4 million in taxable income and also paying interest. The adjustment amount represented the vast majority of the profits in HK Sub during the audit years. The taxpayer also agreed to abide by the 5% markup target for all future years.

Key Enforcement Trends Highlighted by the Case

This case highlights several emerging trends in China’s current tax enforcement landscape that are especially relevant to multinational taxpayers with operations in the region.

First, the taxpayer had secured High and New Technology Enterprise (“HNTE”) status and enjoyed the reduced 15% enterprise income tax (“EIT”) rate. However, Chinese tax authorities increasingly associate HNTEs with entrepreneurial functions. As a result, they tend to question transfer pricing arrangements when an HNTE is seen to capture only a modest share of group-wide profits within the supply chain.

Second, the taxpayer’s pursuit of an offshore claim for its Hong Kong subsidiary (“HK Sub”) limited the ability to maintain commercial substance in Hong Kong. This weakened its transfer pricing position in China, particularly given the authorities’ growing emphasis on substance in low-tax jurisdictions. Because HK Sub lacked personnel performing key functions, the Wuxi tax bureau dismissed risk-based arguments, selected HK Sub as the tested party, re-characterized it from a mini-principal to a limited-risk entity, and allocated residual profits to China. This approach is broadly consistent with guidance from the PRC State Taxation Administration (“STA”) as articulated in the 2017 UN Transfer Pricing Manual.

In addition, since HK Sub indicated that some of its personnel carried out business functions while traveling in China, the Wuxi authorities could have explored a permanent establishment (“PE”) assessment. However, in practice, Chinese tax officials often prefer to rely on transfer pricing adjustments rather than invoke PE arguments when both options are available, likely due to administrative practicality and greater flexibility in asserting taxing rights.

Finally, although the 15% EIT rate under the HNTE regime was generally aligned with Hong Kong’s standard profits tax rate, the taxpayer’s offshore claim effectively blocked access to Mutual Agreement Procedure (“MAP”) relief. Because the Chinese adjustment did not result in actual double taxation, MAP was not available as a defense mechanism. If the taxpayer had wished to challenge the outcome, administrative appeal or litigation would have been the primary courses of action.
