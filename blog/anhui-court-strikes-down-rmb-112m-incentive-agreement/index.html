Anhui Court Strikes Down RMB 11.2M Incentive Agreement

Local subsidy tied to future tax revenues ruled an illegal rebate

MNCs often conduct location studies when making new investments in China and one factor to consider is the incentives offered by the governments in-charge of the investment projects. Recently a litigation case was reported in Anhui province concerning the fulfillment of commitment regarding incentives that were previously agreed upon by the local government. Although the incentives were documented in an investment agreement between the government and the taxpayer, the local government subsequently argued that they lack legal basis and the agreement should not be enforced. The courts agreed.

Summary of the Dispute

The taxpayer was in the real estate development industry. In 2003, the taxpayer entered into an investment agreement with the government of the Lujiang County, Anhui Province. The agreement stipulated that the taxpayer should complete a commercial real estate project on the land offered by the Lujiang government; the Lujiang government in turn should provide financial rebates to the taxpayer equal to 30% of the business tax, 80% of the land appreciation tax, and 40% of the local portion of the enterprise income tax that would be due from the future sale of the developed real estate project. The incentives translated to an actual amount of RMB 11.2 million after the project was finished and sold. Years later, the local government refused to pay the subsidies despite written requests from the taxpayer during the 2015-2017 period. The taxpayer filed lawsuits against the Lujiang government in the Intermediate Court and the Supreme Court of Anhui Province in 2017 and 2018.

When defending its case, the Lujiang government took the position that its promises in the investment agreement lacked legal basis and should not be fulfilled. It cited Article 3 of the PRC Tax Administration Law, which prohibits the local governments from granting tax incentives that deviate from provisions of the national law and regulations, and argued that it made promises beyond its legal authority. The courts agreed and cited several national bulletins from the Chinese State Council disallowing local governments to grant tax rebates that are not specified in the national laws and regulations, unless such local tax rebate policies have been reviewed and approved by the central government. The taxpayer counter-argued that the incentives offered by the Lujiang government were fiscal subsidies rather than tax rebates, and did not fall within the scope of the cited bulletins. The Courts ruled that as the fiscal subsidies are directly tied to the amount of future tax revenue, they are in essence tax rebates and have the effect of altering the national tax rules for selected taxpayers. The final court decisions were in favor of the government.

Key Lessons for MNC Investors

The case highlights the enforcement risks that taxpayers face when negotiating with local governments on incentives. In practice, some local authorities make aggressive offers of incentives when soliciting investment capital from out of their regions. Subsequently, factors, such as a change of leadership team personnel, a deterioration of fiscal conditions, etc., may lead to a reluctance to meet the terms on the part of the local governments in some situations.

While the history of honoring promised incentives in the past in a particular location should certainly be evaluated, even if the local government is willing to implement an investment agreement, the subsidies may still be subject to internal audit by the upper-level authorities and challenged from above. Therefore, during negotiation, it is important to review the nature of the incentives offered by the local government, analyze the terms in the agreement, and assess/anticipate contract risks where appropriate.

On a final note, for most local subsidies, unless they satisfy the specific requirements for exemption, they are subject to PRC enterprise income tax. For these “tax exempt” local subsidies, expenditures that are financed by them may not be deductible for enterprise income tax purposes. MNCs should keep separate ledgers to track these funds and associated expenditures to mitigate risks during tax audits.
