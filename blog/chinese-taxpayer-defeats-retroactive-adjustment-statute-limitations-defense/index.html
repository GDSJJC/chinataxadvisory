Chinese Taxpayer Defeats Retroactive Adjustment with Statute of Limitations Defense

LAT Recalculation Overturned as Court Enforces Statutory Limitation Period

The following case illustrates how the Statute of Limitations (“SOL”) can function as a decisive defense strategy in Chinese tax audits. In this instance, a Chinese taxpayer relied on SOL provisions to challenge the legitimacy of a proposed audit adjustment, and ultimately prevailed. The case underscores that, when invoked effectively, procedural time limits can offer taxpayers a powerful means of protection in navigating complex and retrospective audit disputes in China.

Case Overview

The taxpayer was a Chinese company incorporated in Putian, Fujian Province. The company sold some immovable properties in 2004 and 2005 and was liable to pay the Chinese land appreciation tax (”LAT”), which is a type of excise tax triggered by a disposition of Chinese real estate. In 2006, the taxpayer was audited by the in-charge tax bureau in Putian and received a tax adjustment notice (”2006 notice”) stipulating how much LAT was owed. The taxpayer complied with the 2006 notice and settled the audit by paying the requested amount of tax.

In 2010, the same district tax authorities issued a second notice letter (”2010 notice”) informing the taxpayer that the tax computation method in the 2006 notice turned out to be inconsistent with overarching provincial tax guidance. Therefore, the district tax bureau repealed the 2006 tax notice and re-computed the tax for the 2004-2005 period using a new calculation mechanism, resulting in a much higher tax payable amount. The taxpayer challenged the 2010 notice, and the parties did not work out their differences during the administrative appeal. The taxpayer then sued the government and won the support of the district court. The tax authorities appealed the case but lost it again at the municipal court.

Technical Analysis

One of the main arguments used by the taxpayer to win the case was the SOL. Under the Chinese tax collection and administration law, the SOL for tax adjustment varies by the following circumstances.\

  • The SOL is unlimited for tax evasion and fraud cases.

  • The SOL is ten years for transfer pricing adjustment cases.

  • The SOL is five years for the vast majority of other tax cases in which the tax underpayment was caused by unintentional mistakes made by the taxpayer.

  • If a tax adjustment is due to errors made by the tax authorities in the administration process, the SOL is three years.

Whether a tax adjustment falls within the SOL is determined by measuring the time between the starting date (e.g., the original tax return due date or tax payment due date) and the ending date (e.g., the date of discovering the tax deficiency by the tax authorities). The selection of the starting date and the ending date is often disputed in practice because the Chinese tax laws and regulations do not define them clearly. In the case above, the courts ruled that the starting date of the measurement period is when the tax authorities issued the 2006 tax notice, which fixed the amount of tax owed by the taxpayer under the old computation method. The ending date of the measurement period is when the tax authorities released the 2010 tax notice to change the tax computation method and levy more tax. The exact length of the measurement period here was 3 years, 7 months, and 24 days.

The Chinese courts took the view that as the tax authorities issued the 2010 tax notice to unilaterally change the tax computation method that it previously chose for the taxpayer in the 2006 tax notice, any additional tax due to the change of method was attributable to the action of the government rather than a mistake of the taxpayer. Therefore, the courts considered an SOL of three years to be applicable. Since the measurement period was longer than three years, the courts ruled that the taxpayer was not under any obligation to follow the 2010 notice and increase its tax liability for the 2004-2005 period.

Insights

Audit cases in China frequently involve historical tax positions, making the SOL a critical factor in many disputes. Depending on the specific nature of the issue, such as whether it involves underreported income, failure to file, or fraud, different SOL rules may apply, and the calculation of the applicable time window can become a focal point of contention. In practice, both the starting and ending dates of the SOL measurement period are often vigorously debated between taxpayers and tax authorities.

A well-developed SOL argument can place multinational companies in a stronger negotiating position, and in some cases, may be outcome-determinative. In the case discussed above, for example, the court ruled in favor of the taxpayer without addressing the technical merits of the underlying tax computation, relying solely on the conclusion that the audit had exceeded the applicable limitation period.

Given this dynamic, it is recommended that MNC taxpayers adopt an integrated audit defense strategy, leveraging both technical tax positions and procedural safeguards such as the SOL. This dual-track approach can significantly increase the chances of a favorable resolution in Chinese tax audits.
