Suzhou Bankruptcy Case Turns Transfer Pricing Dispute into Shareholder Lawsuit

Bankruptcy dispute shows how transfer pricing adjustment escalated into shareholder liability

A recent development in China’s tax enforcement landscape has blurred the line between tax administration and civil liability. What began as a routine transfer pricing audit in Suzhou ended with the foreign parent companies of a Chinese subsidiary being held jointly and severally liable in a civil court, an extraordinary outcome with far-reaching implications for multinational groups operating in China.

Case Background: Suzhou Subsidiary Under Audit

The taxpayer was a Chinese entity (“China Sub”) incorporated in the Suzhou Industrial Park in Jiangsu Province. China Sub was wholly owned by a Hong Kong company (“HK Parent”), which in turn was fully owned by the ultimate foreign parent (“Group Parent”) based in another tax jurisdiction. Together, the HK Parent and Group Parent are referred to here as the foreign shareholders.

China Sub operated as a processor of goods: it purchased raw materials from the foreign shareholders, manufactured finished products, and sold those goods back to the foreign shareholders. Its transfer pricing policy used the comparable uncontrolled price (“CUP”) method, referencing prices for similar transactions with unrelated parties.

Audit and Adjustment: From CUP to TNMM

In 2014, China Sub was audited by the No. 4 Tax Investigation Bureau of the Suzhou Industrial Park (“Tax Office”). The auditors challenged the CUP method, arguing that the comparables were not sufficiently reliable. They reclassified China Sub as a limited-risk contract manufacturer, asserting that it had overpaid for raw materials and undercharged for finished goods.

On May 8, 2015, the Tax Office issued a Preliminary Adjustment Notice (“PAN”), replacing the CUP with the Transactional Net Margin Method (“TNMM”). The resulting adjustment increased China Sub’s income by RMB 388 million for the period 2005–2012, triggering RMB 87 million in back taxes and late payment interest. A Final Adjustment Notice (“FAN”) followed on May 15, 2015, confirming the PAN.

Bankruptcy and Creditor Claim

By this time, China Sub was already in financial difficulty and had filed for bankruptcy and debt restructuring with the Suzhou Industrial Park People’s Court (“District Court”). The court accepted the petition and appointed a CPA firm as the bankruptcy administrator.

On the same day the FAN was issued, the Tax Office submitted a creditor claim to the bankruptcy administrator, requesting to be added to the creditor list for RMB 87 million. This claim was recognized by the administrator in December 2015.

Civil Litigation Against the Parent Companies

In a highly unusual development, the bankruptcy administrator filed a civil lawsuit on March 25, 2016 against the foreign shareholders, demanding financial compensation equivalent to the transfer pricing adjustment and interest. The administrator’s argument was that the subsidiary’s insolvency was due to improper pricing policies imposed by the foreign shareholders.

On July 22, 2017, the District Court ruled in favor of the bankruptcy administrator:

  • The Group Parent was held liable for over RMB 400 million in damages.

  • The HK Parent was held jointly and severally liable.

The foreign shareholders appealed to the Suzhou Intermediate Court, but the appeal was rejected on September 28, 2018. The ruling stood.

Key Lessons for Multinational Taxpayers

  • Tax Adjustment Notices Can Become Civil Evidence

In China, a FAN is not just a tax document. It can serve as documentary evidence in civil litigation. In this case, the FAN supported the Tax Office’s creditor claim, and enabled a civil lawsuit to be brought against the foreign shareholders, even though they owned the Chinese entity 100%.

  • Bankruptcy Increases Parent Liability Risks

The foreign shareholders may have assumed that bankruptcy would eliminate China Sub’s tax debts or at least shield them from personal exposure. Instead, it stripped them of control. The appointed administrator, not the shareholders, took charge and used the FAN as the basis for legal action against the shareholders themselves.

  • Audit-Stage Defense Is Critical

During the litigation, the foreign shareholders tried to raise technical arguments, including that China Sub was not a limited-risk entity and retained decision-making authority, and that China Sub bore market risk, including underutilization and inventory costs. But by then, it was too late. These arguments were not raised effectively during the audit. Worse, China Sub had failed to file a formal objection to the PAN within the 7-day period allowed under Chinese law, which left the Tax Office free to issue the FAN uncontested.

  • Passive Cooperation Is a Strategic Mistake

Some taxpayers believe that being “cooperative” during an audit may lead to leniency. That was not the case here. A passive or deferential audit posture does not protect against aggressive assessments. On the contrary, it may embolden auditors. The proper strategy is to challenge flawed logic, documentation, or methodology immediately and thoroughly during the field investigation phase.

Final Thought

This case is a clear warning for multinational companies operating in China: Transfer pricing audits should be treated as litigation in slow motion. The cost of delay or inaction can be extreme, not just in taxes and penalties, but in reputational risk and cross-border legal exposure.

Had the China Sub mounted a more proactive defense and engaged with the PAN immediately, the outcome might have been very different. Instead, a tax adjustment became a bankruptcy-triggered civil lawsuit, and the foreign shareholders were left footing the bill.
