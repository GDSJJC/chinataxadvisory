Xiamen Tax Audit Recasts Service Fees as Deemed Dividends

Substance-over-form doctrine triggers denial of deductions and WHT

A Chinese “deemed dividend” case was recently reported in Xiamen, Fujian Province. While the case started as a transfer pricing (“TP”) investigation, the government expanded it and created deemed dividends to align with the actual balance sheet of the Chinese taxpayer, under the “substance-over-form” doctrine, to collect dividend withholding tax.

Case Background

The Chinese taxpayer is a foreign-invested enterprise (“China Sub”) in the Haicang District of Xiamen city. It was incorporated about 15 years ago and was engaged in the manufacture of electric wires and cables. In April 2017, China Sub entered into a service agreement with three overseas affiliates, under which it would receive a package of support services in the areas of technology, sales, procurement, logistics, and R&D. The TP policy was set at cost plus 5%, and China Sub had paid service fees to the three affiliates periodically since then.

The HDTB audited this transaction and claimed that (1) the intercompany service agreement was vague about the service deliverables and lacked measurement details often found in 3rd party contracts, and (2) the activities per the agreement were too broad in scope and served to exercise control over China Sub rather than to confer commercial benefits. In addition, while the 5% margin itself was moderate, the HDTB considered the cost base loosely defined in the agreement. Due to limitations in the accounting systems, the overseas affiliates were unable to produce breakdowns of the cost base figures behind the invoice amounts. The HDTB challenged that the service transaction deviated from common commercial practices and should not be respected from a TP standpoint.

The arguments so far are not unusual in a Chinese TP audit involving outbound service fees. However, the HDTB was not content with adjusting the tax-deductible amounts of the payments. It escalated the TP audit into a “tax avoidance” investigation and argued that the arrangement represented a covert effort to repatriate cash without paying the Chinese dividend withholding tax.

To align the non-recognition of the intercompany transaction with the actual cash balance of China Sub, the HDTB invoked the “substance-over-form” doctrine and deemed hypothetical dividends. While the tax jurisdiction of China Sub’s foreign shareholder had an income tax treaty with China offering a 5% preferential dividend withholding rate, the HDTB decided that the foreign shareholder did not qualify as a beneficial owner and imposed a 10% withholding tax on the deemed dividends under China’s domestic tax law.

After the two sides negotiated for multiple rounds, China Sub conceded to the deemed dividend treatment. This resulted in both denial of income tax deduction and additional withholding tax and maximized collection for the HDTB.

Key Comments

The HDTB’s attempt to align the actual and the “should-be” cash positions via a deemed dividend treatment is akin to a conforming TP adjustment. However, the Chinese TP rules do not contain a conforming adjustment provision, so the HDTB used “substance-over-form” in the name of anti-tax avoidance as a bypass. When presenting the Chinese legal analysis for aligning the balance sheets and hypothesizing deemed dividends, the HDTB cited the 2017 OECD Model Treaty Convention as support.

Although the HDTB’s approach was aggressive, China Sub could have fought the case better. It could argue that whether general tax avoidance exists is subjective so it should be given fair assessment following the national procedures and any negative conclusion should be subject to a higher burden of proof rather than based on the judgment of the local auditors alone. It could also explore the suitability of using deemed intercompany receivables/payables as an alternative to the deemed dividend treatment for aligning the balance sheets, among other things.

All in all, the case highlights the necessity of good planning for cross-border intercompany transactions to help preempt an escalation of audit into the anti-avoidance area. The key is to enhance the attributes of a transaction according to the intended characterization, keep the form close to the substance, and avoid creating adverse fact patterns that could encourage a recast attempt. This way, the audit would be confined to TP and avert the additional tax impacts associated with a deemed transaction.
