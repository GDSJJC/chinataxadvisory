Partial Tax Deferral Now Available for Qualifying Mergers and Divisions

STA Announcement No. 13 of 2026 ends the sixteen-year all-or-nothing rule: qualifying mergers and divisions may apply special tax treatment to part of the transaction and defer tax on the consenting slice, but only where resident-enterprise shareholders aggregating more than 50 percent consent, every mandatory cohort holder joins that consensus, and for twelve months thereafter the tests continue to hold.

Highlights

Three points stand out in this alert. First, Announcement No. 13 allows qualifying mergers and divisions to apply partial special tax treatment: where resident enterprise shareholders aggregating more than 50 percent consent, the consenting slice may defer tax while the remainder is taxed currently. Second, more than 50 percent is only the first of three conditions; whether the consenting slice defers also depends on whether the mandatory cohorts, being resident enterprise shareholders holding at least 5 percent and the ten largest such holders, join the consensus and refrain from transferring the shares received for twelve consecutive months, and on whether the consenting aggregate remains above 50 percent, and the three conditions stand or fall together. Third, for the general-treatment slice the successor may elect between the standard and the simplified basis methods, and the simplified method is irrevocable once chosen; the transferor still recognizes the full gain on that slice in the restructuring year.

Background: How Special Treatment Works, and How Consensus Blocked It

Chinese reorganizations fall into six statutory forms, of which mergers and divisions are two. Every reorganization defaults to general tax treatment: the deal is taxed as though assets and shares changed hands at fair market value, gains are recognized at once, and the receiving enterprise takes a stepped-up fair-value basis for future depreciation. Simple and final, but capable of pricing a sensible deal out of existence by accelerating tax on unrealized appreciation.

Special treatment is the deferral alternative. Where it applies, the transferor recognizes no gain at restructuring, the receiver inherits the original tax basis, and tax waits until the assets or shares leave the reorganized group. Deferral is not exemption, and it is never automatic. Cai Shui [2009] No. 59 grants it only where five conditions hold together: a reasonable commercial purpose without a principal avoidance motive; the prescribed proportion of assets or equity being acquired, merged, or divided; twelve months of continued historic business after restructuring; an equity-consideration ratio meeting the notice threshold; and a twelve-month lockup during which the original major shareholders receiving equity may not transfer the shares. For mergers and divisions, Article 6 of No. 59 generally requires equity consideration of at least 85 percent of the transaction consideration, with an exception for same-control mergers for which no consideration is paid. Cai Shui [2014] No. 109 later cut the equity-acquisition and asset-acquisition thresholds from 75 to 50 percent and added a safe harbour for transfers within a wholly controlled group, but it did not amend the 85 percent equity-consideration ratio for mergers and divisions, and the architecture of purpose and double twelve-month continuity survived intact.

The binding constraint sat one level down, in administration. Since STA Announcement No. 4 of 2010, parties to one reorganization generally took consistent tax positions. A single dissenting holder could therefore deny deferral to all, and deals with dispersed registers, employee holders, or legacy minorities routinely died at the starting line, restructured, delayed, or abandoned for tax reasons because full agreement was out of reach.

Change One: Partial Deferral Subject to Three Conditions

Announcement No. 13, four articles issued on 8 July 2026 for restructuring dates from 1 January 2026, provides that resident enterprise shareholders (shareholders that are Chinese tax residents) aggregating more than 50 percent may carry the consenting slice into special treatment. More than half means strictly more, measured on the restructuring date. The official explanation, nine Q&As with eight worked examples, reads as a reply to the unanimity history, including a 4 percent holder whose partial transfer sinks the entire structure, evidently chosen to warn against precisely the complacency the new flexibility invites.

But the 50 percent test is only the first of three conditions. Every resident enterprise shareholder at or above 5 percent on the restructuring date, and every one of the ten largest resident enterprise shareholders at any percentage, must join the consensus and must not transfer the shares received for twelve consecutive months. Breach by any mandatory holder kills special treatment for the whole transaction. Smaller consenting holders face a collective rather than individual test with the same consequence: transfers leaving the consenting aggregate at 50 percent or below fail the mechanism transaction-wide. The register must therefore cover every consenting holder rather than the mandatory names alone.

Shareholder-level treatment, third, remains outside enterprise-level special treatment. Individuals, nonresident enterprises, partnerships, and contractual asset management products may be shareholders of the merged or divided enterprise, but they stay outside special EIT treatment under their own rules and never count toward the 50 percent threshold. Article 3 of Announcement No. 13 expressly brings partnerships, contractual asset-management products, and nonresident enterprises into the permitted shareholder universe, building on Announcement No. 48 of 2015, which had already admitted individuals. Enterprise-level partial application is the actual reform, so a register containing founders, funds, or offshore holders permanently runs on two tracks.

A hypothetical shows how the three conditions interact (assumptions: Company A absorbs Company B, both resident enterprises; restructuring date 30 June 2026; all No. 59 conditions satisfied except consensus). Shareholder Jia, a resident enterprise holding 52 percent, consents, shareholder Yi, a resident enterprise holding 6 percent, stays silent, and the remaining minority holders totaling 42 percent consent. Consenting interests reach 94 percent, far above half, yet Yi's silence as a mandatory 5-percent holder vetoes the whole transaction. Reverse the facts: Yi joins while a 4 percent holder transfers half its shares in month ten, dropping the consenting aggregate to 50 percent or below, and the structure fails all the same, with every party that used special treatment required to adjust. None of the three conditions may be missed.

Change Two: The Irrevocable Basis Election for the General-Treatment Slice

The general-treatment slice forces an economic choice worth modeling rather than defaulting. Under the standard method, each asset and liability in the general portion takes fair value, yielding higher basis and larger later depreciation and amortization. Under the simplified method, original basis is retained and the aggregate fair-value difference becomes a separate asset recovered evenly over ten years starting with the restructuring year. The election is irrevocable, and suitability turns on disposal expectations, depreciation profiles, and asset mix. Land, buildings, and intangibles with different remaining lives will answer differently, and loss-position taxpayers should be especially careful, since a fixed recovery rhythm may suit gain positions better than loss ones.

One asymmetry should be stated plainly. The simplified method changes only the successor's deductions. The transferor still recognizes the full gain on the general slice in the restructuring year. Models blending both sides of the transaction will overstate the benefit, and accounting carrying amounts should be kept strictly apart from tax basis so deferred-tax effects trace to the elected treatment rather than to merger bookkeeping.

Change Three: Scope, Date, and the Limits of the Official Examples

The remaining boundaries are straightforward but unforgiving. The substantive No. 59 conditions as amended by No. 109 continue for any slice seeking special treatment. The more-than-50-percent resident-enterprise consent threshold in Announcement No. 13 is a separate gate from the equity-consideration ratio in No. 59, which for mergers and divisions is generally at least 85 percent; both must be met for the consenting slice to defer. The 50 percent threshold introduced by No. 109 applies to equity and asset acquisitions, which sit outside the partial mechanism. Equity acquisitions, asset acquisitions, debt restructurings, and similar arrangements sit outside the partial mechanism however merger-like they appear commercially. Other administration continues under Announcements No. 4 of 2010 and No. 48 of 2015, with No. 4 partly repealed by No. 48, so each point needs checking rather than assuming either document governs whole. The restructuring date follows Announcement No. 48 and the explanation rather than signing or announcement dates, which makes transactions straddling 1 January 2026 a documentation exercise first and a tax conclusion second.

Impact and What to Do

Taken together, the highlights resolve into a single message: the reform turns blocked deals into manageable ones, and what those deals now demand is precision on the shareholder register. Before selecting the partial method, confirm the steps constitute a merger or division, fix the restructuring date under Announcement No. 48, map every holder by residence, form, percentage, and top-ten status, and record who must consent, who has consented, and which interests sit in each slice. Retest all substantive conditions with a contemporaneous commercial-purpose memorandum. At closing, hold consents in the restructuring agreement or a separate protocol since no form is prescribed, residence and entity-status support, the capitalization table, transaction documents, valuation, consideration bridge, date evidence, the special-versus-general allocation, and return positions. After closing, maintain holder monitoring, transfer restrictions, shareholder communications, and reporting controls for the full twelve months, with notification, return-correction, indemnity, and document-support duties agreed before any transfer surfaces rather than after. No consolidated filing form or local manual for the partial method has been issued, so prepare supporting files as though the reviewer will ask for everything. Under the new monitoring logic that reviewer arrives after closing rather than before, and the benefit belongs to shareholder precision, a supportable basis bridge, and continuing compliance.

Filing forms, local implementation, and early practice on the separate asset will be covered as they emerge.
