Shenzhen Pioneers Joint Tax–Customs Program on Transfer Pricing

New pilot mechanism aligns APA and customs rulings to reduce double taxation

On May 18, 2022, the Shenzhen State Tax Bureau and the Shenzhen Customs Authority jointly issued Bulletin 2022-62 (“Bulletin 62”), launching a new mechanism aimed at coordinating transfer pricing (“TP”) and customs import valuation. This Coordinated Management Program (“CMP”) allows the two authorities and eligible taxpayers to sign a tripartite memorandum, aligning pricing methods across both regulatory domains.

While currently limited to taxpayers registered in Shenzhen, the CMP could serve as a pilot for national rollout, a familiar path for new regulatory tools in China, where Shenzhen frequently functions as a test bed for tax modernization and administrative reform.

Historical Context: The Transfer Pricing–Customs Divide

For years, multinational groups operating in China have struggled with conflicting expectations between tax and customs authorities on related-party import pricing. The State Taxation Administration (“STA”) and General Administration of Customs (“GAC”) have developed separate systems, Advance Pricing Arrangements (“APA”) under STA Bulletin 2016-64, and Customs Advance Rulings (“CAR”) under GAC Order 236, each designed to provide upfront certainty. However, the two systems operate independently, and neither includes a mechanism for mutual recognition.

This lack of coordination creates the risk of double taxation or double adjustments, especially in cases involving year-end pricing true-ups, which may be justified under TP norms but viewed skeptically by customs officers. Even when a taxpayer obtains an APA or CAR, there is no guarantee the other agency will accept the pricing outcome.

The New Mechanism: Bridging the Gap

The CMP seeks to eliminate this structural gap by offering a unified process for taxpayers to engage both authorities simultaneously. Once a taxpayer is accepted into the program, the tax and customs agencies conduct a joint review of the proposed pricing policy. If alignment is reached, the parties enter into a Memorandum of Coordinated Management (“MCM”), supported by a formal APA and customs advance ruling, each attached to the MCM.

The MCM sets out the key pricing elements: the applicable TP method, the customs valuation approach, the arm’s length range (or fair transaction value range), and the median to which the taxpayer must conform annually. Notably, taxpayers must adjust to the median, not simply remain within range, adding a degree of precision to implementation.

The CMP is valid for three years, with the option for renewal. Participating taxpayers must submit annual implementation reports within six months of each year-end.

Eligibility and Application Process

To qualify for the CMP, the taxpayer must:

  • Be registered in Shenzhen;

  • Already be eligible for both an APA and a CAR under existing programs;

  • Submit a complete joint application to the Shenzhen tax and customs authorities;

  • Cooperate in joint or separate due diligence as required.

Applications are reviewed for completeness within 10 working days, and once accepted, the evaluation proceeds to joint fact-finding and negotiation. If consensus is reached, the MCM, APA, and CAR are finalized as one coordinated package.

Strategic Implications for Multinationals

From a transfer pricing standpoint, the CMP can be viewed as an enhanced APA, extending its protective scope beyond income tax adjustments to cover customs treatment as well. For taxpayers, this dual-track coverage offers a critical advantage: the counterbalancing interests of the two authorities may create a more neutral negotiating environment, compared to dealing with either agency alone.

Although Bulletin 62 does not specify whether the APA must be unilateral or bilateral, a unilateral arrangement is far more likely in practice. Including a foreign tax authority in the process would dilute the autonomy of Chinese agencies and complicate coordination, undermining the intended streamlining effect of the CMP.

For multinational taxpayers, particularly those with import-heavy supply chains involving affiliated parties, the CMP represents a promising new tool for managing pricing certainty and mitigating double taxation risks, challenges that have historically required parallel and often duplicative efforts with tax and customs offices.

A Local Program with National Potential

While currently limited to Shenzhen, the CMP is designed with scalability in mind. Its launch reflects broader trends in China’s tax administration, including a shift toward cross-agency collaboration, improved taxpayer services, and incentives for investment compliance.

Given Shenzhen’s role as a pilot zone for national reforms, MNEs operating outside the city should closely monitor the program’s rollout and impact. If successful, the CMP could serve as a template for replication in other first-tier cities or national-level economic zones.

Recommendations

Multinational taxpayers should consider the following:

  • If registered in Shenzhen, evaluate whether your group qualifies for the CMP and whether it could enhance your pricing certainty and internal controls.

  • If operating outside Shenzhen, monitor developments and assess whether your local authorities may be amenable to a similar structure. In some cases, companies may wish to advocate proactively for the adoption of comparable frameworks.

  • Review existing or planned APA and customs ruling applications to identify overlap and assess whether joint resolution would be more efficient or defensible under scrutiny.

  • Consider engaging in early dialogue with both tax and customs authorities to explore how future pricing policies might be structured for coordinated review.

Final Thought

The Shenzhen CMP reflects a growing recognition among Chinese authorities that multinational pricing disputes cannot be resolved in silos. By bridging the divide between transfer pricing and customs enforcement, this pilot offers a pragmatic path forward for companies seeking predictability, compliance, and relief from double taxation exposure.

If it proves successful, it could mark the beginning of a new era in inter-agency coordination, one in which taxpayers benefit from clearer rules, fewer surprises, and a more unified regulatory approach to cross-border pricing.
