STA's Draft Conduct Rules for Tax Officials

Proposed Draft Reemphasizes Enforcement Boundaries and Signals Continued Move Toward Rule-Based Administration

INTRODUCTION

China maintains one of the world’s largest tax-administration workforces, reflecting the scale and operational reach of its nationwide collection and enforcement system. Last month, the State Taxation Administration (“STA”) released a draft update to its internal framework on compliance by tax officials, and invited public comments. By prescribing disciplinary actions for non-compliant behaviors, the draft indirectly illustrates to the public the internally mandated boundaries that tax officials are expected to observe in their collection, audit, and enforcement work.

For businesses, understanding these boundaries helps in assessing how to strategically communicate with the local tax authorities, whether when receiving requests, disagreeing with an assessment, or managing a dispute. It also enables companies to judge when a matter may have substantive implications or risks for their interests and how to assert their rights.

HIGHLIGHTS

1. Timing of Collection

A central message of the draft is that tax enforcement must follow legal timing, legal basis, and proper procedure, not administrative pressure or informal arrangements. In day-to-day practice, internal revenue targets sometimes influence when local officials seek to collect tax. The draft indicates that officials should neither accelerate collection to meet internal targets nor defer collection simply because current-year targets have been satisfied. Both artificial prepayment and deferral are treated as inconsistent with proper collection practices.

Accordingly, if a taxpayer is asked to “help the bureau close the year” by prepaying tax, or is requested to delay payment for administrative convenience (which may create late payment risks for the company), the draft confirms that such requests fall outside acceptable conduct from a regulatory standpoint.

2. Legal Basis of Tax Treatment

The draft underscores that major tax decisions should rest on statutory/regulatory support. Actions such as initiating or suspending the collection of a tax, exemptions, refunds, etc., should have a clear legal basis. Administrative convenience or local interpretation may not substitute for legal authority. For businesses, this means that when confronted with an adjustment request that appears informal or unsupported, it is reasonable to ask for the detailed legal grounds.

The draft also addresses local development incentives. In some locations, investment-attraction packages rely on creative tax-related arrangements that sit in grey areas. The draft states that tax officials may not disregard non-compliant incentives initiated by the local government to attract investment, nor may they assist in designing or implementing them. This reinforces that incentive commitments dependent on informal cooperation may not be reliable, and companies should evaluate whether an incentive offer is grounded in legitimate policy or relies on actions officials are not permitted to take.

3. Procedural Boundaries and Audit Conduct

The draft reinforces procedural safeguards within the tax audit process. If an official has a potential conflict, such as personal ties to the taxpayer or prior involvement in the matter, the official must withdraw from the case.

Closely related is the prohibition on retribution. If a taxpayer disagrees with an audit outcome, requests clarification, or files a lawful appeal, officials may not respond with suggestions of further investigation or warnings against exercising rights.

The draft also confirms that a failure to perform required duties can itself constitute a violation. Neglecting to act where mandated by the law and regulations to do so may result in internal consequences for officials. This helps explain why some officials may adopt very cautious positions in ambiguous areas, not necessarily due to hostility toward taxpayers, but out of concern for avoiding internal errors. Recognizing this dynamic can help businesses interpret certain interactions with greater accuracy.

TAKEAWAY

While the rules are in draft, they offer a sense of how STA expects tax enforcement to function in practice. Understanding this expectation can provide taxpayers a frame of reference for interpreting day-to-day interactions with the local tax authorities. When the underlying rules and expectations are better understood, companies can more accurately assess the nature of a request or disagreement, and plan their responses with more intention. This often leads to more stable and predictable management of communications with the local tax authorities in practice.
