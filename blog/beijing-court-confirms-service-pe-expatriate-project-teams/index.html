Beijing Court Confirms Service PE from Expatriate Project Teams

Aggregated short-term visits deemed to exceed treaty threshold

A recent tax investigation case in Beijing clarifies a common misunderstanding regarding Permanent Establishment (“PE”) exposure under Chinese law. The case illustrates how frequent visits by international travelers, even when each individual’s stay in China is brief, can collectively trigger PE risks.

Background of the Investigation

In the case, the Chinese company was a foreign investment enterprise (“FIE”) formed in the Chaoyang District of Beijing. It entered into a consultancy contract with a German company under which it would receive technical consulting services over a period of two years. When fulfilling the contract, the German entity dispatched a total of 32 expatriates into China and offered on-site advisory support to the FIE.

More than two years after the consultancy contract was completed and payment was made, the Chaoyang tax investigation team picked up the transaction and examined the potential Chinese individual income tax (“IIT”) liabilities of the 32 expatriates. All the salary compensation of the expatriates was paid by the German entity outside China. Each expatriate stayed in China for fewer than 183 days during the term of the contract. The taxpayer took the position that the presence of the expatriates in China did not create a “Service PE” under Article 5(3)(b) of the China-Germany Income Tax Treaty (“DTA”) because they were not in China long enough. Article 15 (“Income from Employment”) of the DTA stipulates that the expatriates should not be liable to Chinese IIT if (1) they stayed in China for fewer than 183 days within 12 months; and (2) their compensation was not paid by a Chinese entity or borne by a Chinese PE.

The tax auditors challenged the no-PE position. They stated that the “Service PE” provision in the DTA should be assessed on a project-by-project basis. Even though each of the expatriate spent fewer than 183 days in China, their presence in China was all linked to the execution of the consultancy contract and therefore should be aggregated. The collective presence of the expatriate team would breach the “183 days out of a 12-month period” threshold in Article 5(3)(b) of the DTA. If a Service PE arose, a part of the expatriates’ salaries would be considered as borne by the Service PE even though the cash compensation was paid by the German entity outside China. Based on this position, the Chaoyang District tax authorities levied IIT and late payment interest on the expatriates’ compensation corresponding to their length of stay in China.

Lessons for Multinationals

This case illustrates a long-standing position held by China’s central tax authorities regarding the creation of a Service Permanent Establishment (“Service PE”) through the presence of foreign personnel in China. Several key takeaways emerge:

To begin with, although individual income tax (“IIT”) was ultimately imposed on the non-resident individuals involved, the foreign-invested enterprise (“FIE”) that engaged them via a consultancy contract likely acted as the withholding agent. As such, the FIE was financially liable for both the tax shortfall and related interest. This underscores the importance for FIEs to proactively assess PE risks associated with their overseas service providers and to ensure that indemnification clauses are built into consultancy agreements to safeguard their financial exposure.

Once a Service PE is deemed to arise, it remains in existence for the duration of the consultancy project, regardless of how frequently or infrequently foreign personnel are physically present in China. Even a single day of presence during the project period could result in the expatriate’s compensation being attributed to the Service PE and thereby subject to Chinese IIT.

In this particular case, due to the involvement of a government investigation team with a specific mandate, the authorities focused only on individual income tax implications. However, once a Service PE is established, the foreign service provider, in this case, the German entity, may also become liable for Chinese enterprise income tax on its business profits from the engagement. Again, the FIE could be held responsible for withholding and remitting this tax.

Finally, from a broader compliance perspective, multinational groups should note that China’s tax authorities may aggregate the travel days of multiple foreign service team members across a single project to determine whether a Service PE has been created. Individual travel duration alone does not control the PE analysis. It is therefore critical that travel schedules are centrally coordinated and carefully monitored to manage the overall risk of triggering a Service PE.
