Ningbo JV Faces $7M Transfer Pricing Settlement After Audit Clash

Authorities reject loss defense and enforce positive TNMM markup

A recent anti-avoidance investigation in Ningbo, Zhejiang Province highlights the growing intensity of Chinese tax authorities’ scrutiny over transfer pricing (“TP”) compliance, particularly in cases involving related-party transactions and persistently low profitability.

Case Background

The case centered on a joint venture (“JV”) between a Chinese company and a Japanese multinational enterprise, with registered capital of approximately USD 40 million. The JV operated under a buy-sell model and was primarily engaged in the assembly and testing of electronic devices.

Following 2009, the JV experienced a fourfold increase in sales. However, its profitability remained consistently low, as noted by the Ningbo tax authorities in their review of the taxpayer’s annual TP reports. The full cost markup of the JV during the periods of review was on average 1.76%, in contrast to 5.89% among local electronics manufacturers. Most of the JV’s revenue was from related party sales.

The low profit margin triggered a TP audit on the JV. The tax investigators in Ningbo conducted on-site functional analysis interviews with the various departments of the taxpayer and inspected the assembly and testing operation of the JV multiple times. The tax officials also contacted the 3rd party suppliers and final customers in the overall supply chain to verify certain information received during the interviews, such as product characteristics and bargaining powers, etc.

Key Issues in the Audit

During the negotiation, the two sides focused on the justification of the losses that the taxpayer incurred in certain years. The taxpayer argued that factors such as heavy market competition, challenges in product quality, high costs in raw materials, etc. squeezed profitability in the supply chain. Losses of the taxpayer were caused by these uncontrollable business factors rather than a non-arm’s length TP policy.

The tax authorities asserted that the rapid expansion of sales by the taxpayer during the audit period suggested the products possessed good competitive advantages and growth potential in the market. Furthermore, the tax officials contended that as the taxpayer performed no R&D functions and took on no market risks, it was not an entrepreneur and should not bear losses in the supply chain.

After rounds of negotiations, the taxpayer ultimately agreed to adjust its FCMUs to a positive target level for the years under audit using TNMM, and also accepted the tax bureau’s position on the comparable set as well as computation details such as determination of the full cost base, etc. The settlement plan was approved by the central tax authorities. The amount of tax and interest settled was approximately USD $7 million.

Lessons for Multinationals

This case offers several important insights into how Chinese tax authorities approach transfer pricing audits, and how taxpayers can better prepare for such scrutiny:

To start, local transfer pricing reports were the primary trigger for audit selection. The Ningbo tax authorities relied on standardized checklists and procedures to identify potential targets. This underscores the importance of proactively addressing likely challenges in the TP documentation itself. Anticipating the authorities’ concerns, and responding convincingly within the report, can materially reduce the risk of being selected for audit.

In addition, consistency of facts from the outset is critical. The authorities did not accept the taxpayer’s representations at face value. Instead, they sought to independently verify the factual assertions made in the TP reports through multiple external sources. This highlights the need for careful coordination across all documentation, communications, and disclosures.

Another key consideration is the treatment of limited-risk entities. Where a company is characterized as such, Chinese tax authorities generally expect it to earn a positive return each year, regardless of whether it is in a startup phase. There is no assumed grace period. Taxpayers must be prepared to defend low or negative margins with robust, fact-specific justification.

Furthermore, JV structures may not offer the protection some taxpayers assume. While the existence of a joint venture may suggest an internal check-and-balance dynamic between partners, Chinese tax authorities typically do not treat this as a sufficient safeguard. They tend to take a broad view of related-party arrangements and will apply TP principles accordingly, without presuming arm’s-length behavior from JV structures alone.

Finally, technical rigor remains the best line of defense. Methodology and comparables can heavily influence the size of a TP adjustment. Even small concessions in these areas can have a disproportionate impact on the final number. When disagreements arise, taxpayers should not hesitate to invoke the technical framework provided by both Chinese TP rules and OECD guidelines. A clear, methodical defense grounded in regulatory principles offers the strongest path to a favorable resolution in the Chinese audit context.
