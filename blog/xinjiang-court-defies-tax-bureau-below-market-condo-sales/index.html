Xinjiang Court Defies Tax Bureau on Below-Market Condo Sales

Judges reject turnover tax adjustment, uphold taxpayer’s pricing discretion

An unusual tax controversy litigation from the Xinjiang region offers important precedent on the limits of tax authority discretion, particularly regarding price adjustments between unrelated parties. The case centered on whether Chinese tax authorities could revise the pricing of a real estate transaction that was not between related parties, and ultimately, the courts ruled in favor of the taxpayer.

Case Background

The case started as a tax audit launched by the district tax bureau in 2011 against the taxpayer for its 2009-2010 tax years. At the end of the audit, the tax authorities handed out various tax adjustments, all of which were conceded by the taxpayer except for one item. Specifically, the taxpayer sold some residential condominiums to certain individual buyers, who were retired employees of a group affiliate entity in the same city (”Target Transaction”). Compared with similar residential units sold in the local market around the same time, the selling price in the Target Transaction was lower by approximately 20%.

The district auditors claimed that the taxpayer charged below-market prices for the sale of the condos and therefore underpaid Chinese turnover tax on the transaction. The taxpayer disagreed with the assessment. The case was escalated to the district court and the appellate court, where the government was defeated in both instances.

The case was not about transfer pricing because the taxpayer and the individual condominium buyers were not related parties under the Chinese transfer pricing regulations. The type of tax involved in the Target Transaction was the “business tax”, a type of turnover tax in existence before the VAT reform, rather than income tax. Under Article 35 of the Chinese Tax Collection and Administration Law (”CTCAL”), the tax authorities have broad power to adjust the transaction price for computing tax liabilities even if the transaction is between 3rd parties, as long as the transaction price is found to deviate from the market price without a “proper reason.”

During the court proceedings, the taxpayer argued that as an independently operating business it has the discretion over its commercial pricing policy. It explained that the retired employees of its group affiliate were lower-income earners and often lodged group complaint with the local government authorities for not receiving proper social welfare coverage. The taxpayer offered a 20% discount in the sale of the condos to the retired employees as a placating measure for social stability and that constituted a “proper reason” within the scope of the CTCAL provision cited above. Both courts agreed to this argument.

It is noteworthy that when the case was being debated in the appellate court, the local tax authorities sent a formal request for guidance to the STA, the Chinese central tax authorities in Beijing. The STA issued two letter rulings indicating support to the local tax authorities’ position in the case, and they were submitted to the appellate court as regulatory support for the local government’s position in the litigation. The court ruled that the letter rulings were internal communications within the tax bureau hierarchy and did not have a binding legal force outside the tax bureaus.

Key Takeaways

Although China does not follow a common law system, and no single court decision is binding on future cases, each judicial outcome is closely watched by the tax community and often provides valuable guidance for future audit defense strategies.

Two notable insights emerge from the Xinjiang case:

First, even when transactions are conducted between unrelated parties, Chinese tax authorities may still invoke Article 35 of the China Tax Collection and Administration Law (“CTCAL”) to adjust pricing and recalculate tax liabilities, if the agreed transaction price materially deviates from prevailing market levels. This type of adjustment is more commonly observed in non-income tax categories, such as deed tax or real estate VAT. In such cases, the taxpayer’s best line of defense often centers on the “proper reason” exception embedded in the statutory language of Article 35.

Second, not all regulatory issuances from the State Taxation Administration (“STA”) carry equal weight in litigation. While STA letter rulings with broad, general applicability may be persuasive, those issued in response to specific fact patterns or inquiries from lower-level tax authorities are not automatically binding on courts. As illustrated in this case, the court may reject such rulings if the legal reasoning or underlying facts are unconvincing. This underscores a critical reality: the mere existence of a written document issued by the STA does not guarantee judicial deference.
