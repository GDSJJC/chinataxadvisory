China Royalties Face Double Tax Exposure in Customs–Tax Clash

MNCs hit by both withholding tax and import VAT as authorities diverge

When a Chinese company pays royalties to a foreign enterprise, these payments are often scrutinized for transfer pricing compliance if the two parties are related. However, even when no related-party relationship exists, royalties can still become subject to unintended double taxation, not due to pricing issues, but due to a lack of coordination between China’s tax and customs authorities.

This alert explores how royalty payments can give rise to duplicative tax liabilities and outlines how multinational companies can proactively manage the risk.

Institutional Divide: Separate Rules, Separate Authorities

In China, the tax authority and the customs authority operate under different administrative systems. The State Taxation Administration governs income tax and value-added tax (excluding import-related VAT), while the General Administration of Customs oversees tariffs and import-related value-added tax.

Each authority issues its own rules and interpretations, and in practice, local tax bureaus and customs offices may take conflicting views on how royalties should be taxed. This structural misalignment can result in duplicated taxation on the same royalty payment, even when such an outcome is not intended under law.

A Common Scenario: Royalties in Cross-Border Product Sales

Consider the following scenario:

  • A Chinese company imports tangible goods from a foreign seller.

  • Separately, it pays a royalty to that same seller, perhaps for use of trademarks, patents, or technology associated with the goods.

If the royalty is treated as a non-trade payment, the Chinese company will typically withhold income tax at 10 percent and value-added tax at 6 percent at the time of payment. Under certain tax treaties or exemptions, these amounts may be reduced, but in a standard case, both taxes are withheld and remitted to the in-charge tax bureau.

Problems arise when the customs authority later audits the transaction and determines that the royalty is not a stand-alone license fee, but instead is “related to” the imported goods, either directly or as a condition for their sale into China. In that case, customs may assess import-related value-added tax and tariff, treating the royalty as part of the dutiable value of the imported goods.

The Economic Problem: When Two Authorities Tax the Same Royalty

Technically, Chinese law contemplates that a royalty payment should be taxed either as a trade-related item (by customs) or as a non-trade payment (by the tax authority), but not both.

However, in practice, the Chinese company usually settles the withholding taxes when it makes the royalty payment. Months or even years later, the customs authority may retroactively assess import taxes on the same royalty, asserting that the original classification was incorrect.

If the Chinese company complies and pays the import taxes, it rarely succeeds in reclaiming the taxes already withheld and paid to the tax bureau. This results in duplicative taxation, in form if not strictly in law.

Some argue that this is not technically double taxation because different parties are taxed: the foreign enterprise is subject to income tax and value-added tax (via withholding), while the Chinese importer is responsible for tariffs and import value-added tax. But from an economic standpoint, the same payment is taxed twice, and the total cost of the transaction increases. Whether or not it meets the textbook definition of double taxation, the financial burden is real.

Practical Risk: More Common Than It Seems

This issue arises more frequently than many companies expect, especially in jurisdictions where customs offices initiate systematic audits of royalty payments. The financial exposure can be significant, as royalty-related reassessments may cover multiple years and large transaction volumes.

Recommendations for Multinational Companies

To mitigate the risk of duplicative taxation on royalty payments, multinational taxpayers should consider the following steps:

  • Conduct a forward-looking legal and factual review of license arrangements, payment terms, and how these relate to the importation of goods.

  • Draft royalty and license agreements with precision, clearly articulating the scope, purpose, and independence of the royalty from product pricing.

  • Anticipate customs interpretations of what constitutes a royalty “related to” imported goods.

  • Consider pre-transaction consultations with the in-charge tax bureau and local customs office in high-risk cases.

  • Maintain documentation that supports the separate nature of the royalty in both economic and legal terms.

Being proactive in structuring and documenting royalty arrangements is essential. The disconnect between China’s tax and customs systems is unlikely to be resolved in the near term, and multinational companies must manage the risk through foresight and careful planning.
