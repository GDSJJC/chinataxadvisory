Japan DTA Case: PE Triggered by Combining Installation Contracts in Shenzhen

Authorities aggregate service days across two projects, imposing EIT and IIT

The following summarizes a 2019 case published by China’s central tax authorities, which sheds light on how the aggregation of contracts can influence the determination of a service permanent establishment (“PE”).

Case Background

In this case, a Chinese company imported seven pieces of high-valued equipment from a Japanese MNC for approximately US$ 23 million under two sales contracts in June 2014. The Japanese MNC (”Seller”) did not pay any Chinese enterprise income tax (”EIT”) on the basis that the income from the equipment sale was not China-sourced and was not attributable to a Chinese PE. The Chinese tax authorities investigated the transactions and found that the installation process was fairly complex and required extensive on-the-ground assistance from the Seller in the PRC.

While the sales contracts did not earmark any of the sales consideration as compensation for the installation services, the Chinese tax authorities argued that a portion of the sales price should be for the installation functions performed by the Seller, which were an integral part of the equipment sales. The tax officials allocated approximately 10% of the sales proceeds as the implicit installation service revenue realized by the Seller. As the installation activities largely took place in China through the Seller’s traveling personnel, the associated income (determined on a deemed basis) was considered to be China-sourced under the PRC EIT law.

Points of Dispute

Technically, whether the installation service income should be subject to Chinese EIT mainly depends on whether it was attributed to a service PE under the China-Japan income tax treaty. In most Chinese income tax treaties, a service PE can arise when foreign personnel stay in China for longer than a “threshold period” while rendering services for a project. That “threshold period” is typically six months or 183 days within 12 months on a rolling basis. The China-Japan income tax treaty (”DTA”) contains such a provision.

The Seller argued that since the parties entered into TWO equipment sales contracts, the PE assessment should be made in the context of each sales agreement on a stand-alone basis. The Chinese tax authorities checked the entry records provided by the PRC border control administration authorities and verified that a total of 15 people from the Seller were sent into China to assist in the installation. They spent 165 days for one sales contract and 167 days for the other. Since the length of stay under each contract was below the threshold period under the DTA, the Seller believed that it did not have any service PE in China.

Audit Determination

The tax authorities counter-argued that one project may correspond to multiple inter-related contracts. Through onsite investigations, the tax authorities learned that the seven pieces of equipment were installed at the same physical facility and their roles complement each other in the manufacturing process of the Chinese importer; the 15 technical personnel of the Seller managed and supervised the installation of all the equipment under the two contracts. Considering that the transactional parties were the same in the two importations, the government concluded that the two contracts should be aggregated for assessing whether the “threshold period” is exceeded.

Under this substance-over-form principle, it was counted that the Seller’s personnel spent 239 days in China between the two contracts, starting from the 1st visit in 2014 to the last departure in 2017. Therefore, the Chinese tax authorities deemed that the Japanese seller had a PE in China and owed EIT. Due to the PE determination, the government also imposed Chinese individual income tax (”IIT”) on the 15 employees from the Seller. The amount of IIT payment was about three times the EIT assessment. Penalty interest was levied too.

Key Takeaways

This case underscores several important considerations for multinational companies selling equipment into China, particularly when on-the-ground installation or after-sales services are involved:

First, Chinese tax authorities may recharacterize part of the equipment sale proceeds as service income, triggering scrutiny under the service permanent establishment (“PE”) rules. This is especially likely when the installation process is complex or requires extended in-country presence by foreign personnel.

Second, in assessing whether a service PE exists, the authorities may aggregate multiple contracts, even if they are contractually separate, if the total time spent in China by the non-resident’s service team exceeds the applicable threshold period. This approach allows them to view the project holistically, rather than in isolation by contract.

Finally, once a service PE is deemed to exist, the tax implications go beyond enterprise income tax (“EIT”). There may also be individual income tax (“IIT”) exposure for the non-resident personnel involved. The resulting tax costs can be significant.
