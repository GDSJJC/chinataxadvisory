Nanjing JV Faces RMB-Billion Transfer Pricing Audit Fallout

Failed MAP leaves joint venture absorbing $180M tax burden

In China’s enterprise income tax (EIT) regime, intercompany transfers of equity in Chinese entities require careful consideration of both fair market value (FMV) and tax basis. While determining FMV typically attracts more attention—especially in a transfer pricing context—accurately establishing tax basis is equally essential for determining the seller’s Chinese income tax liability and ensuring appropriate gain recognition.

For Chinese tax purposes, the seller’s income tax liability is calculated as the difference between FMV and tax basis, multiplied by the applicable tax rate: generally 25% for Chinese tax resident enterprises, and 10% for non-resident enterprises without a permanent establishment.

Note: The discussion below refers to Chinese enterprise income tax unless otherwise specified. It does not reflect preferential EIT rates, tax treaty relief, or deferral provisions under special tax regimes.

Legal Framework for Tax Basis in Equity Transfers

Chinese tax law generally provides that when an enterprise transfers equity previously acquired via purchase, its tax basis is the historical cost, i.e., the actual price paid during acquisition. This is codified in:

  • Article 56 of the Implementation Regulations of the EIT Law (IRCTL), which defines tax basis as “historical cost,” meaning the actual amount paid by the enterprise to acquire the asset.

  • STA Announcement [2017] No. 37, which reaffirms that the tax basis of a non-resident enterprise selling Chinese equity is the actual purchase price paid in the prior acquisition.

In practice, the seller may be required to prove the purchase price—especially under audit—to support its tax basis. The amount paid by the current buyer becomes its own tax basis for future transfers.

Diverging Views in Intercompany Contexts: The Basis Adjustment Question

In intercompany transfers, mismatches often arise between the contractually agreed price and the value reported for tax purposes. For example, related parties may:

  • Set a contract price below FMV for cash flow reasons, while the seller reports FMV to mitigate transfer pricing risks.

  • Face a post-transaction transfer pricing adjustment by tax authorities, raising the effective “deemed” transfer price above what was actually paid.

This leads to a critical question:

If the seller pays tax on an upward-adjusted transfer price, but the buyer makes no corresponding payment, what tax basis should the buyer use in its future resale?

Two Competing Interpretations

View 1: Adjusted Tax Basis (Substance-over-form approach)

If the prior seller paid tax on an adjusted higher price, the buyer should inherit this higher tax basis—even if it did not actually remit the full amount.

View 2: Actual Payment Basis (Legal-form approach)

The buyer’s tax basis should be strictly limited to the amount it actually paid, regardless of the seller’s tax reporting position or subsequent adjustments.

The following scenario illustrates a post-transaction pricing adjustment in an intercompany equity transfer, and the competing views regarding the determination of tax basis.

In 2020, Company A, a non-resident enterprise subject to a 10% EIT rate, transferred its entire 100% equity interest in a Chinese subsidiary, Company C, to its related party, Company B. Company B is a Chinese tax resident enterprise taxed at the standard 25% EIT rate. The agreed transfer price was RMB 50 million.

At the time of the transaction, Company A’s tax basis in the equity was RMB 30 million. This resulted in a recognized gain of RMB 20 million and an initial tax liability of RMB 2 million (i.e., 10% of the gain).

In 2021, the Chinese tax authorities conducted a transfer pricing audit and concluded that the 2020 intercompany sale was not conducted at arm’s length. They adjusted the transfer price upward by RMB 10 million, determining that the fair market value should have been RMB 60 million. Consequently, Company A was assessed an additional tax of RMB 1 million, reflecting the 10% tax on the incremental RMB 10 million gain. Notably, despite this adjustment, Company B did not compensate Company A for the RMB 10 million pricing difference.

Two years later, in 2023, Company B sold its shares in Company C to an unrelated third party, Company D, for RMB 80 million.

Under View 1: Company B may adopt the adjusted RMB 60 million as its tax basis, resulting in a taxable gain of RMB 20 million and a tax liability of (80M − 60M) × 25% = RMB 5 million.

Under View 2: Company B’s tax basis remains the original RMB 50 million it actually paid, resulting in a taxable gain of RMB 30 million and a tax liability of (80M − 50M) × 25% = RMB 7.5 million.

Legal Analysis

Historical Cost Principle in the IRCTL

Article 56 of the IRCTL and STA Bulletin 37 both define tax basis as “historical cost”—the actual acquisition price. On its face, this would support View 2, suggesting that a buyer’s tax basis cannot exceed the amount it paid.

Restructuring Provisions under Bulletin 59

Some argue that Bulletin [2015] No. 59, which governs the tax treatment of equity restructurings, supports View 1. Specifically, Section 1.3 of Bulletin 59 provides that under general tax treatment, the buyer’s basis is FMV. However, this clause implicitly assumes that the transaction was actually executed at FMV.

Therefore, unless the parties transact at FMV (and not merely report FMV for tax), Bulletin 59 does not necessarily override the historical cost principle for setting tax basis.

Equity and Anti-Double Taxation Arguments

While legal text may favor View 2, practical fairness concerns support View 1. If the seller is taxed on an adjusted value but the buyer cannot adopt that value as its basis, double taxation arises on the same gain—first at the seller level, then again upon the buyer’s resale.

To mitigate this risk, many tax practitioners and local tax authorities lean toward substance-over-form logic, allowing the buyer to use the adjusted price as basis, even without additional payment.

Practical and Compliance Considerations

The divergence between View 1 and View 2 stems from the non-symmetric nature of Chinese transfer pricing enforcement:

  • Transfer pricing adjustments do not require accounting or cash flow adjustments between parties.

  • As a result, buyer and seller may hold mismatched views on tax basis, especially when governed by different tax authorities.

Under View 2, if a buyer adopts the higher adjusted price as basis, it faces audit risks unless it can demonstrate actual payment.

Recommendations and Planning Options

To secure a step-up in tax basis, buyers may consider:

  • Supplementary payment to the seller post-adjustment, aligning actual payment with the adjusted transfer price.

  • Retaining detailed records, including remittance proofs, to support future tax positions.

  • Where a supplementary payment is not feasible (e.g., due to FX controls or cash constraints), proactively engaging with the local tax authority may clarify the acceptable approach.

Anticipating these interpretive differences and coordinating across related-party tax positions can reduce the risk of disputes and unintended double taxation in future exits.
