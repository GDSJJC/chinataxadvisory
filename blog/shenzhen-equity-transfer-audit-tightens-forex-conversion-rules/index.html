A recently reported VAT audit case in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province highlights the narrow and enforcement-driven interpretation Chinese tax authorities may apply when evaluating VAT exemptions for exported services. The case offers an important cautionary tale for consulting and service companies engaged in cross-border work with offshore clients, particularly when such services involve tangible goods originating from China.

Case Background

The taxpayer in this case was a Chinese consulting firm that provided procurement-related advisory services to a foreign customer, assisting with the sourcing of inventory from suppliers in China. Believing the services qualified for VAT exemption under SAT Bulletin 2016-36, the taxpayer applied a zero-rated VAT treatment on its service income.

Under Bulletin 2016-36, outbound consulting services provided by a Chinese entity to a non-Chinese recipient may be exempt from VAT if the services are “consumed entirely outside of China.” However, the regulation also specifies that services “related to tangible goods or immovable property located in China” are not eligible for exemption, even if the service recipient is overseas.

During a random audit, the Shenzhen tax authorities challenged the exemption position. They argued that the advisory work was inherently linked to physical goods located in China, specifically the inventory being sourced, and thus failed the “consumption outside China” requirement. After several rounds of negotiation, the authorities ruled against the taxpayer and imposed a 6% VAT plus local surcharges on the revenue earned from the foreign client.

Key Technical Considerations

China’s VAT rules for exported services are generally aligned with the OECD’s destination principle, which allocates taxing rights to the jurisdiction where services are effectively consumed. However, SAT Bulletin 2016-36 contains complex carve-outs, and the phrase “consumed outside China” is often interpreted strictly by frontline tax officials.

Key issues for taxpayers to consider include:

  • Substance over form: The fact that the client is located overseas is not sufficient. If the service itself is connected to tangible goods, infrastructure, or land in China, it may still be considered domestically consumed and thus subject to VAT.

  • Local variation in enforcement: While the Bulletin provides national guidance, local interpretations vary, and some local bureaus adopt narrower views than others. What qualifies for exemption in one jurisdiction may be disallowed in another.

  • Risk of recharacterization: Services like procurement support, inspection, quality control, or logistics coordination may be viewed as closely tied to Chinese-based goods, triggering VAT liability even when performed for offshore clients.

Compliance Reminders

If a taxpayer intends to apply a VAT exemption for exported services under Bulletin 2016-36, the following compliance steps are critical:

  • Timely documentation filing: The taxpayer must submit supporting documents to the in-charge tax bureau within the first VAT filing period that covers the service transaction. Failing to do so may result in automatic denial of the exemption, regardless of substantive eligibility.

  • Input VAT credit treatment: Once service revenue is treated as VAT-exempt, associated input VAT is non-creditable. The taxpayer must transfer out the input VAT balance from the tax credit ledger and recognize it as an expense in the income statement.

  • Technical position paper: Before taking an exemption position, companies are strongly advised to prepare a technical memo or internal analysis evaluating the service under current regulatory language and local audit practices. This can serve both as a filing rationale and an audit defense tool.

Final Observations

This Shenzhen case illustrates that exported service exemptions are not automatic, even where the recipient is offshore. Chinese VAT authorities continue to closely scrutinize the substance of cross-border service arrangements, particularly where there is any nexus to goods located in China.

For consulting, sourcing, and advisory firms working with foreign clients, early-stage risk assessment and compliance planning are essential. A cautious and well-documented approach to Bulletin 2016-36 can help reduce the risk of retroactive assessments and preserve access to China’s preferential treatment for qualified exported services.
